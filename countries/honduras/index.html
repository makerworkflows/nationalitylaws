Dual Citizenship

Conditional — Honduras permits dual citizenship under specific circumstances laid out in its Constitution and nationality law.

The 2003 constitutional reform (Decree No. 24-2003, amending Article 24) significantly expanded Honduras’s acceptance of dual nationality. Under the reformed provisions:

  • Central American nationals (Guatemala, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, Panama, Belize) who acquire Honduran nationality, and vice versa, are explicitly permitted to hold both citizenships.
  • Bilateral treaty countries — Honduras has entered into dual nationality agreements with several nations, including Spain. Citizens of these countries may hold dual nationality with Honduras.
  • Hondurans by birth who acquire a foreign citizenship do not automatically lose their Honduran nationality under the reformed constitutional framework. The 2003 reform was designed to protect the large Honduran diaspora (particularly in the United States), ensuring that Hondurans who naturalized abroad could retain their birth nationality.

Key nuance: While the Constitution protects birth nationals from involuntary loss, naturalized Hondurans face stricter rules. Naturalized citizens who voluntarily acquire another nationality may lose their Honduran citizenship.

Practical note: Honduras does not aggressively enforce single-nationality requirements. Many Hondurans in the United States hold both nationalities without issue, though the legal framework is not as clean-cut as countries with unconditional dual citizenship.

Acquisition of Nationality

By Birth (Jus Soli)

Honduras applies unrestricted jus soli. Any person born on Honduran territory acquires Honduran nationality by birth, regardless of the nationality of their parents.

Constitutional basis: Article 23(1) of the Constitution of the Republic of Honduras (1982, as amended).

This includes:

  • Children born anywhere within Honduran national territory, including the mainland, the Bay Islands (Islas de la Bahia), the Swan Islands (Islas del Cisne), and all territorial waters and airspace
  • Children born on Honduran-flagged vessels or aircraft, whether in Honduran or international waters/airspace (Article 23(2))
  • Foundlings (children of unknown parentage found on Honduran territory) are presumed Honduran until proven otherwise

By Descent (Jus Sanguinis)

Honduran nationality by birth extends to children born abroad to Honduran parents:

ScenarioRequirementConstitutional Basis
Born abroad to a Honduran father or motherAt least one parent is Honduran by birth or naturalizationArt. 23(3)
Born aboard Honduran vessels or aircraftAutomaticArt. 23(2)
Child of Honduran diplomatic or consular officials abroadAutomaticArt. 23(3)

Registration requirement: Children born abroad to Honduran parents should be registered at a Honduran consulate. While there is no strict deadline, timely registration facilitates the documentation of nationality. The parent must present proof of their own Honduran nationality (birth certificate, passport, or cedula de identidad).

Generational reach: The Constitution does not impose a generational limit. However, in practice, individuals beyond the first generation born abroad may face additional documentation requirements to demonstrate the unbroken chain of Honduran parentage.

By Adoption

Adoption does not automatically confer Honduran nationality. However:

  • A foreign child adopted by Honduran nationals may acquire Honduran nationality if the adoption is finalized under Honduran law and the child is a minor
  • The adopted child would need to be registered and may need to go through a simplified naturalization process depending on their age and circumstances
  • Under Article 24 of the Constitution, adopted minors of Honduran parents may be treated as nationals by birth

By Marriage

Marriage to a Honduran national does not automatically confer nationality. However, foreign spouses receive preferential treatment for naturalization:

Preferred Pathways

Honduras extends significant preferential treatment to nationals of Central American and Ibero-American countries, reflecting deep cultural, historical, and political ties.

CA-4 Free Movement Zone

Honduras is a founding member of the Central America-4 Border Control Agreement (CA-4), signed in June 2006 in Guatemala City. The CA-4 allows citizens of the four member states to travel freely within the zone without a passport (national ID is sufficient) and to stay for up to 90 days without immigration formalities.

Beyond free movement: CA-4 nationals benefit from the fastest naturalization pathway in Honduras — only 1 year of residency, compared to 3 years for most other nationalities. Additionally, CA-4 nationals can work and conduct business in Honduras with minimal bureaucratic hurdles, reflecting the regional integration goals of the Central American Integration System (SICA).

Special Clauses & Programs

Central American Unity Provisions

The Honduran Constitution contains unique provisions reflecting the country’s commitment to Central American reunification. Article 15 states that Honduras supports and promotes the reconstruction of the Central American Union, and the preferential treatment of Central Americans throughout nationality law reflects this constitutional aspiration.

Honorary Nationality

The Honduran government may grant honorary nationality to distinguished foreigners who have rendered exceptional services to the country. This is distinct from naturalization and is granted by special decree of the National Congress.

Protection of the Diaspora

Honduras has one of the largest diasporas relative to its population, with an estimated 1-1.5 million Hondurans living in the United States alone. The 2003 constitutional reforms were primarily motivated by the desire to protect these emigrants, ensuring they could naturalize in their host countries without losing their Honduran identity and rights, including the right to own property and vote.

Indigenous and Afro-Honduran Communities

Honduras recognizes nine indigenous and Afro-descendant peoples (Lenca, Miskito, Tolupan, Chorti, Pech, Tawahka, Garifuna, Bay Island Creoles, and Nahua). Members of these communities who may have been born outside Honduras but can demonstrate ethnic and cultural ties may have facilitated pathways to nationality recognition.

No Citizenship by Investment Program

Honduras does not offer a formal citizenship-by-investment (CBI) or “golden passport” program. See Investment Pathways below for residency options.

Investment Pathways

Citizenship by Investment (CBI)

Not available. Honduras does not offer a direct path from investment to citizenship. All naturalization requires physical residency in Honduras for the prescribed period.

Residency by Investment (RBI)

Available. Honduras offers residency to foreign investors and retirees through several pathways:

PathwayRequirementsVisa TypeNotes
Pensionado/Rentista (Retiree)Proof of monthly pension or income of $1,500+ USDResidency permitPopular with North American and European retirees; income from foreign pension, Social Security, or investments

Real estate purchasePurchase of residential propertyTemporary residencyProperty must be in the applicant’s name; Bay Islands (Roatan) is a popular destination
Business establishmentFormation and operation of a Honduran companyResidency permitMust demonstrate ongoing business activity and employment of Honduran nationals

Path to citizenship: After obtaining residency, the standard naturalization timeline applies — 3 years for most nationalities, 1 year for Central Americans, Spaniards, and Ibero-Americans. The residency must be continuous and legal.

Roatan and the Bay Islands: The Bay Islands, particularly Roatan, have become a significant draw for foreign investors and retirees due to lower costs of living, Caribbean lifestyle, English-speaking communities (Bay Island Creole), and growing tourism infrastructure. Property ownership by foreigners is permitted throughout Honduras, including coastal and border areas (unlike some other Central American countries).

Business & Entrepreneurship

Honduras offers a low-cost operating environment and strategic geographic position between North and South America, with Atlantic and Pacific access. However, security concerns, institutional weakness, corruption (Transparency International score of 23), and limited infrastructure outside major cities significantly constrain the business environment. The country has been working to attract foreign investment through special economic zones and tax incentives.

Company Formation

Most common entity typeSociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada (S. de R.L.) — Limited Liability Company
Formation timeline2-4 weeks
Formation costL 15,000-40,000 HNL (~USD 600-1,600)
Minimum capitalL 5,000 HNL (~USD 200) for S. de R.L.
Online registrationLimited — notarization and in-person steps required

The S. de R.L. (limited liability company) is the most common structure for small and medium businesses. For larger operations, the Sociedad Anonima (S.A.) (corporation) is used. Formation requires a notary public (notario), registration with the Camara de Comercio e Industrias (Chamber of Commerce), and obtaining a tax identification number (RTN — Registro Tributario Nacional). Foreign nationals can own 100% of a Honduran company in most sectors.

Other entity types include:

  • S.A. de C.V. (Variable Capital Corporation) — for businesses expecting capital changes
  • Comerciante Individual — sole proprietorship (simplest form, no separate legal entity)
  • Branch office — foreign companies can register a branch (sucursal) in Honduras

Entrepreneur & Startup Visas

Honduras does not have a dedicated startup or entrepreneur visa. Common pathways for foreign entrepreneurs:

  • Investor Residency: For those investing $50,000+ USD in a Honduran business; grants residency with work authorization
  • Pensionado/Rentista: If the entrepreneur has passive income of $1,500+/month from abroad; can engage in business activities
  • Temporary Residency: General temporary residency can be obtained through employment by a Honduran company, including one the applicant owns
  • CA-4 Nationals: Citizens of Guatemala, El Salvador, and Nicaragua can work and operate businesses with minimal immigration formalities

Special Economic Zones

Honduras has been at the forefront of special economic zone experimentation in Central America:

ZEDEs (Zonas de Empleo y Desarrollo Economico) — Employment and Economic Development Zones were created under a 2013 constitutional amendment. These zones were designed to operate with their own administrative, fiscal, and regulatory frameworks to attract foreign investment. However, the ZEDE program was repealed in April 2022 by the Xiomara Castro government. Existing ZEDEs (notably Prospera on Roatan and Ciudad Morazan near Choloma) are in legal dispute regarding their status and the rights of existing investors.

ZOLIs (Zonas Libres / Free Zones) remain active and provide significant tax incentives:

IncentiveDetail
Corporate income taxExempt for the first 20 years
Municipal taxesExempt
Import dutiesExempt on raw materials, machinery, and equipment
ISV (VAT)Exempt on inputs
Export taxesExempt

ZIPs (Zonas Industriales de Procesamiento) — Industrial Processing Zones offer similar benefits for export-oriented manufacturing, particularly in the maquila (assembly/manufacturing) sector:

  • Honduras is Central America’s largest maquiladora hub
  • Dominant sectors: textiles/apparel, automotive wire harnesses, and agricultural processing
  • Tax holidays of 10-20 years for qualifying operations

Banking & Financial Infrastructure

  • Major banks: Banco Atlantida (largest), BAC Honduras, Banco Ficohsa, Banpais, Banco de Occidente, Davivienda Honduras
  • Central bank: Banco Central de Honduras (BCH)
  • Currency: Honduran Lempira (HNL); USD is widely accepted in tourist areas and the Bay Islands
  • Exchange controls: Honduras has moderate foreign exchange controls; large transactions require documentation
  • Digital banking: Limited but growing; mobile banking adoption increasing through bank apps
  • Stripe: Not natively available in Honduras; businesses use regional payment processors or route through US entities
  • Remittances: Honduras receives approximately $7-8 billion USD annually in remittances (roughly 25% of GDP), primarily from the United States
  • Opening a business bank account: Requires RTN (tax ID), company registration documents, proof of address, personal identification; the process can take 1-3 weeks

Key Programs & Incentives

  • COALIANZA / CONAPID — Public-private partnership agency promoting investment in infrastructure and development projects
  • FIDE (Fundacion para la Inversion y Desarrollo de Exportaciones) — The primary investment promotion agency, assists foreign investors with setup, permits, and incentives
  • ProHonduras — Government agency promoting exports and investment
  • Free Zones (ZOLIs) — 20-year tax holidays for qualifying businesses (see above)
  • Maquila regime — Preferential tax treatment for export-oriented manufacturers
  • Tourism incentives — Tax breaks for hotel and tourism infrastructure investment, particularly in the Bay Islands, Copan, and La Ceiba corridor
  • San Pedro Sula — Industrial and commercial capital; largest concentration of manufacturing, banking, and commerce
  • Tegucigalpa — Political capital; government services and growing services sector
  • Roatan / Bay Islands — Tourism hub; growing expat community, English-speaking, real estate investment
  • DR-CAFTA — Dominican Republic-Central America Free Trade Agreement provides preferential access to the US market for qualifying Honduran exports

Loss of Nationality

Voluntary Renunciation

  • Nationals by birth: May renounce Honduran nationality by formal declaration before competent authorities. However, the 2003 constitutional reform significantly protects birth nationals, and in practice, renunciation is rare and difficult to enforce.
  • Naturalized citizens: May voluntarily renounce Honduran nationality by formal declaration.

Involuntary Loss

Nationals by birth: Under the 2003 constitutional reform, Honduran nationality acquired by birth is strongly protected. A Honduran by birth does not lose their nationality by acquiring another citizenship. However, the Constitution still contains provisions under which nationality could theoretically be lost:

CauseApplies ToDetail
Voluntary acquisition of another nationalityNaturalized citizensNaturalized Hondurans who voluntarily acquire a third-country citizenship may lose Honduran nationality (unless covered by a dual nationality agreement)
Treason or acts against the stateBoth birth and naturalizedEngaging in treason, espionage, or acts against the sovereignty of Honduras
Serving a foreign government without authorizationNaturalized citizensActing as an official or agent of a foreign state without permission from the Honduran government
Fraud in naturalizationNaturalized citizensIf naturalization was obtained through false documents or misrepresentation
Extended absenceNaturalized citizensProlonged absence from Honduras without maintaining ties (rarely enforced)

Naturalized citizens face stricter provisions than birth nationals. The asymmetry mirrors the approach of most Latin American countries, where naturalized citizenship carries additional conditions.

Practical Guide

Documents Needed

For naturalization by residency (standard checklist):

  • Completed naturalization application form
  • Valid passport (original + certified copy)
  • Current Honduran residency permit (permiso de residencia) — original + copy
  • Birth certificate (original, apostilled or legalized, translated into Spanish by a certified translator)
  • Criminal background check from country of origin (apostilled, translated into Spanish)
  • Criminal background check from Honduras (issued by the Direccion de Investigacion Criminal, DIC)
  • Certificate of good conduct (constancia de buena conducta)
  • Two passport-sized photographs (white background, front-facing)
  • Proof of legal means of livelihood (employment letter, business registration, bank statements, tax returns)
  • Proof of continuous residency in Honduras (utility bills, lease agreements, bank statements)
  • Proof of entry into Honduras (immigration stamps, visa)
  • Marriage certificate (if applying under marriage category — apostilled, translated)
  • Spouse’s Honduran nationality documentation (if applying under marriage category)
  • Medical certificate from a Honduran physician
  • Payment receipt for application fee
  • RTN (Registro Tributario Nacional — tax ID number)

For registration of nationality by descent (born abroad to Honduran parents):

  • Parent’s Honduran birth certificate or proof of Honduran nationality
  • Applicant’s birth certificate (apostilled, translated)
  • Parent’s Honduran passport or cedula de identidad
  • Two passport-sized photographs
  • Application submitted at Honduran consulate or National Registry of Persons

Related Countries

Countries with preferential nationality and immigration relationships with Honduras:

  • Guatemala — CA-4 partner; free movement with ID; 1-year naturalization; shared border and deep cultural ties; fellow SICA member
  • El Salvador — CA-4 partner; free movement with ID; 1-year naturalization; fellow SICA member
  • Nicaragua — CA-4 partner; free movement with ID; 1-year naturalization; shared border; fellow SICA member
  • Mexico — Ibero-American preferential category (1-year naturalization for Hondurans in Mexico and vice versa); significant Honduran migrant community in transit and residence
