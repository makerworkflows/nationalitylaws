Dual Citizenship

Yes — Mexico has recognized dual (and multiple) nationality since the 1998 constitutional reform; Mexican nationals who acquire another citizenship no longer lose their Mexican nationality.

Prior to March 20, 1998, acquiring a foreign citizenship caused automatic loss of Mexican nationality. The 1997 amendment to Article 37 of the Constitution (effective March 20, 1998) eliminated involuntary loss of nationality by birth. Mexicans who lost their nationality before that date may recover it by filing a declaration before SRE.

Key distinction: Mexico differentiates between nacionalidad (nationality) and ciudadania (citizenship). Nationality is the legal bond; citizenship is the exercise of political rights. Naturalized Mexicans who acquire another nationality after naturalizing may lose their Mexican nationality (see Loss of Nationality below). Mexican nationals by birth cannot lose their nationality.

Acquisition of Nationality

By Birth (Jus Soli)

Mexico applies unrestricted jus soli. Any person born on Mexican territory acquires Mexican nationality by birth, regardless of the nationality of their parents.

Constitutional basis: Article 30(A)(I) of the Political Constitution of the United Mexican States.

This includes:

  • Children born anywhere within Mexican national territory (including territorial waters and airspace)
  • Children born on Mexican-flagged vessels or aircraft (Article 30(A)(III))

By Descent (Jus Sanguinis)

Mexican nationality by birth also extends to children born abroad to Mexican parents:

ScenarioRequirementConstitutional Basis
Born abroad to a Mexican father or mother (by birth)At least one parent is Mexican national by birthArt. 30(A)(II)
Born abroad to a Mexican father or mother (by naturalization)At least one parent is naturalized MexicanArt. 30(A)(II)
Born on Mexican vessels or aircraftAutomaticArt. 30(A)(III)

Registration requirement: Children born abroad to Mexican parents must be registered at a Mexican consulate. There is no generational limit explicitly stated in the Constitution, but in practice SRE requires proof of the parent’s Mexican nationality. A child born abroad to a Mexican-born-abroad parent may need to demonstrate the chain of nationality.

No expiration: There is no deadline to register. Adults who discover they have a claim to Mexican nationality by descent can file at any age.

By Adoption

Adoption does not automatically confer Mexican nationality. However:

  • A foreign child adopted by Mexican nationals may apply for naturalization with reduced residency requirements
  • Minor children of a person who naturalizes as Mexican can also be included in the naturalization process (if they reside in Mexico)

By Marriage

Marriage to a Mexican national does not automatically confer nationality. However, it significantly reduces the residency requirement for naturalization:

Special Clauses & Programs

Recovery of Mexican Nationality

Mexicans who lost their nationality before the 1998 reform (by acquiring foreign citizenship prior to March 20, 1998) may recover it by filing a Declaratoria de Nacionalidad Mexicana por Nacimiento at any SRE office or Mexican consulate. This is a declaration, not a naturalization — they are recognized as having always been Mexican nationals by birth.

Nationality for Notable Contributions

Under Article 20(IV) of the Ley de Nacionalidad, the Secretariat of Foreign Affairs may grant naturalization with reduced or waived residency requirements to foreigners who have made outstanding contributions to Mexico in cultural, scientific, technical, artistic, sporting, or business domains.

Indigenous and Historical Communities

Mexico does not have a formal ancestry/repatriation program, but descendants of Mexican nationals (regardless of generation) may pursue nationality by descent if they can document the chain of Mexican parentage.

No Citizenship by Investment Program

Mexico does not offer a formal citizenship-by-investment (CBI) or “golden passport” program. See Investment Pathways below for residency options.

Investment Pathways

Citizenship by Investment (CBI)

Not available. Mexico does not offer a direct path from investment to citizenship. All naturalization requires physical residency and passing the language/culture exams.

Golden Visa / Residency by Investment (RBI)

Available (indirectly). Mexico offers residencia temporal and residencia permanente based on economic solvency, which can serve as a pathway to eventual naturalization:

PathwayRequirementsVisa TypeNotes
Economic solvency (income)Proof of monthly income ~$5,300+ USD (or equivalent) for the last 6 monthsResidente Temporal (1-4 years)Based on INM thresholds, updated annually
Economic solvency (savings)Bank balance of ~$31,000+ USD for the last 12 monthsResidente Temporal (1-4 years)Based on INM thresholds, updated annually
Real estate ownershipProperty investment of ~$380,000+ USDResidente Temporal (1-4 years)Threshold varies by INM guidelines
Business investmentInvestment in Mexican business, variesResidente Temporal (1-4 years)Must demonstrate job creation or economic benefit
Points-based systemProfessional/technical qualifications + economic activityResidente PermanenteVaries case-by-case

Important: Income and savings thresholds are set by the Instituto Nacional de Migracion (INM) and are updated annually based on the Mexican daily minimum wage (Unidad de Medida y Actualizacion, UMA). The figures above are approximate for 2025-2026. Always verify current thresholds at INM.

Path to citizenship: Residencia Temporal (1-4 years) can be converted to Residencia Permanente, and after accumulating the required years of legal residency (5 standard, or 2 for reduced categories), the resident may apply for naturalization.

Business & Entrepreneurship

Mexico is experiencing a historic nearshoring boom as global companies relocate supply chains closer to the US market, creating unprecedented opportunities for entrepreneurs. The low cost of operations, proximity to the world’s largest economy, and a young workforce are major draws. However, regulatory complexity, security concerns in some regions, and a still-developing startup financing ecosystem temper the overall score.

Company Formation

Most common entity typeS. de R.L. de C.V. (Sociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada de Capital Variable)
Formation timeline2-6 weeks
Formation costMXN 15,000-30,000 (~USD 850-1,700)
Minimum capitalMXN 3,000 (~USD 170)
Online registrationPartial — notarization required

The S. de R.L. de C.V. (variable capital LLC) is the most common structure for SMEs and startups. The S.A. de C.V. (variable capital corporation) is used for larger companies or those seeking external investment. Formation requires a notario publico (public notary), making the process slower and more expensive than in the US. You need a RFC (Registro Federal de Contribuyentes) — Mexico’s tax ID. Foreign nationals can own 100% of a Mexican company in most sectors, though some activities near borders and coastlines have restrictions under the Foreign Investment Law.

The SAS (Sociedad por Acciones Simplificada) was introduced as a simplified one-person corporation with no notary required and zero minimum capital, but has seen limited adoption due to revenue caps (MXN 5M) and restrictions.

Entrepreneur & Startup Visas

Mexico does not have a dedicated startup visa. Common pathways:

  • Temporary Resident Visa (Residente Temporal): Can engage in business activities; requires proof of economic solvency (monthly income of ~USD 2,600+ or savings of ~USD 43,000+). 1-4 year duration
  • Temporary Resident — Economic Activity: Allows employment and business management; requires a Mexican company as sponsor
  • Permanent Resident (Residente Permanente): Unrestricted work rights; requires 4 years as temporary resident, or significant income/pension threshold
  • NAFTA/USMCA Professional Visa (TN): For US and Canadian professionals in specific categories (not entrepreneur-specific but useful for consultants)

Mexico’s immigration system is relatively flexible — temporary residency allows broad economic activity, and the income thresholds are low by international standards.

Tax Overview

Personal income tax (top rate)35% (above MXN 4.5M)

The RESICO (Regimen Simplificado de Confianza) is a game-changer for small businesses and freelancers: a simplified tax regime with rates of just 1-2.5% on gross income for individuals earning up to MXN 3.5M (~USD 200K). The IMMEX (Maquiladora) program provides VAT and duty exemptions for manufacturers exporting from Mexico.

Mexico’s 30% corporate rate is higher than many competitors, but operating costs (labor, rent, utilities) are dramatically lower than the US or Europe, often resulting in higher net margins.

Loss of Nationality

Voluntary Renunciation

  • Nationals by birth: Cannot lose their Mexican nationality involuntarily. However, they may voluntarily renounce it only while abroad and following formal procedures. In practice, Mexico makes it very difficult for birth nationals to renounce — the 1998 reform was specifically designed to prevent loss of nationality.
  • Naturalized citizens: May voluntarily renounce by formal declaration before SRE.

Involuntary Loss

Nationals by birth: Under Article 37(A) of the Constitution (as amended in 1997), Mexican nationality acquired by birth is irrevocable. It cannot be lost for any reason, including:

  • Acquiring another nationality
  • Serving in a foreign government
  • Using a foreign passport

Naturalized citizens may lose their Mexican nationality in the following cases (Article 37(B)):

CauseDetail
Acquiring a subsequent foreign nationalityVoluntarily obtaining citizenship of another country after naturalization
Serving a foreign government without SRE permissionActing as an agent or official of a foreign state without authorization
Using a foreign passportUsing a foreign nationality document before Mexican authorities
Residing abroad for 5+ consecutive yearsLiving outside Mexico for a continuous period of 5 years (rebuttable)
FraudIf the naturalization was obtained through false information or fraud

Note on naturalized citizens: The restrictions on naturalized Mexicans are notably stricter than those on birth nationals. This asymmetry is a deliberate constitutional design. Naturalized Mexicans who wish to travel on another passport or engage with foreign governments should consult SRE first.

Practical Guide

Documents Needed

For naturalization by residency (standard checklist):

  • Completed application form (Solicitud de Carta de Naturalizacion)
  • Valid passport (original + copy)
  • Current Mexican residency card (tarjeta de residente temporal or permanente) — original + copy
  • Proof of legal entry into Mexico (immigration stamps, FMM, or equivalent)
  • Proof of residency duration (utility bills, lease agreements, bank statements showing continuous residence)
  • Birth certificate (original, apostilled or legalized, translated into Spanish by a perito traductor)
  • Criminal background check from country of origin (apostilled, translated)
  • Criminal background check from Mexico (issued by state attorney general’s office)
  • Two passport-sized photographs (white background, front-facing)
  • Marriage certificate (if applying under marriage category — apostilled, translated)
  • Spouse’s Mexican nationality documentation (if applying under marriage category)
  • Birth certificates of Mexican children (if applying under parent-of-Mexican-child category)
  • Proof of financial solvency (bank statements, employment letter, tax returns)
  • Payment receipt for application fee (Derechos)
  • CURP of spouse or Mexican family member (if applicable)

For recovery of nationality by birth:

  • Completed Solicitud de Declaratoria de Nacionalidad Mexicana por Nacimiento
  • Proof of Mexican birth (birth certificate) OR proof of Mexican parentage (parent’s birth certificate, parent’s Mexican passport, etc.)
  • Valid foreign passport or ID
  • Two passport-sized photographs

Exams: The Spanish language exam and Mexican history/culture exam are administered in person at SRE offices. The culture exam covers Mexican history (pre-Columbian through modern), geography, national symbols, civics, and the Constitution. Study materials are available from SRE.
