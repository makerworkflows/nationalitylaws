Dual Citizenship

Kosovo fully permits dual and multiple citizenship. The 2008 Law on Citizenship (and its 2013 successor) explicitly allow Kosovar citizens to hold one or more foreign nationalities without any effect on their Kosovar citizenship.

There is no requirement to notify Kosovar authorities when acquiring a foreign citizenship, and no renunciation of prior nationality is required when naturalizing in Kosovo.

Practical note: Kosovo’s independence (declared February 17, 2008) is recognized by over 100 UN member states, including the United States, most EU members, and major Western democracies. However, Serbia, Russia, China, and several other states do not recognize Kosovo’s statehood. This affects the international utility of a Kosovar passport — it is not accepted for entry into non-recognizing states, and holders frequently maintain a second passport for travel purposes.

Acquisition of Nationality

By Birth (Jus Sanguinis with Limited Jus Soli)

A child acquires Kosovar citizenship at birth if:

  1. Both parents are Kosovar citizens at the time of birth, regardless of birthplace.
  2. One parent is a Kosovar citizen and the child is born in Kosovo.
  3. One parent is a Kosovar citizen and the child is born abroad, provided the child is registered with a Kosovar diplomatic or consular mission before turning 18.

Limited jus soli: A child born in Kosovo to non-citizen parents acquires Kosovar citizenship only if the child would otherwise be stateless.

By Descent

Kosovar citizenship by descent follows through either parent. Children born abroad to a Kosovar parent must be registered at a Kosovar consulate or upon the family’s return to Kosovo. There is no explicit generational limit, but the parent must have been a citizen at the time of the child’s birth.

Persons who were habitual residents of Kosovo and held citizenship under the former Yugoslavia or under UNMIK-administered Kosovo are recognized as initial citizens of Kosovo under the 2008 declaration of independence.

Documentation challenges: Many Kosovar citizens born before 2008 have civil registry records from the Yugoslav era or the UNMIK administration period. These records were transferred to Kosovar institutions after independence but may be incomplete, particularly for persons displaced during the 1998—1999 conflict. The Civil Registration Agency has worked with UNHCR to reconstruct missing records, but gaps remain, especially for Roma and Ashkali communities.

By Adoption

A foreign child under 18 adopted by a Kosovar citizen acquires Kosovar citizenship upon the finalization of the adoption under Kosovar law. The adoption must be processed through Kosovar courts or recognized by Kosovar authorities if completed abroad.

By Marriage

Marriage to a Kosovar citizen does not confer automatic citizenship. A foreign spouse may apply for naturalization with a reduced residency requirement:

  • 3 years of lawful residence in Kosovo (reduced from 5 years), AND
  • 3 years of marriage to a Kosovar citizen.
  • Must meet all other naturalization conditions.

Special provisions:

  • Persons who have rendered exceptional services to Kosovo (science, culture, sports, economy, or national interest) may be granted citizenship by decision of the President, without meeting the standard residency or other conditions.
  • Ethnic Albanians from neighboring countries (Albania, North Macedonia, Serbia, Montenegro) have historically been among the primary naturalization applicants.

Recognition of initial citizens: All persons who were habitual residents of Kosovo on 1 January 1998, and persons who were habitual residents at any time between 1 January 1998 and the declaration of independence on 17 February 2008 (including refugees and internally displaced persons from Kosovo), were recognized as initial citizens of the Republic of Kosovo. This provision was critical for establishing the citizenry of the new state, particularly for the Serbian minority, Roma community, and other non-Albanian populations.

Passport limitations: The Kosovo passport is not recognized by Serbia, Russia, China, India, and several dozen other UN member states that do not recognize Kosovo’s independence. Kosovar citizens traveling to non-recognizing countries typically use a second passport. Kosovo is also not a member of Interpol or the United Nations, which limits certain international administrative functions.

Loss and Renunciation

Voluntary Renunciation

A Kosovar citizen may renounce citizenship by written application to the Ministry of Internal Affairs, provided:

  1. The person is at least 18 years old.
  2. The person holds or has been assured another citizenship.
  3. The person has no outstanding obligations to the Kosovar state.

Involuntary Loss

Kosovar citizenship may be revoked if:

  • The citizenship was obtained through fraud, false information, or concealment of material facts.
  • The person voluntarily acquired Kosovar citizenship and subsequently engages in acts gravely prejudicial to the vital interests of Kosovo.

Note: Acquiring a foreign nationality does not result in loss of Kosovar citizenship.

Restoration of Nationality

Former Kosovar citizens who lost citizenship through renunciation may apply for restoration. The application is submitted to the Ministry of Internal Affairs. The standard naturalization requirements generally apply, but the residency period may be reduced or waived based on the circumstances of the prior loss and the applicant’s connection to Kosovo.

Stateless Persons and Foundlings

  • A child found on Kosovar territory whose parents are unknown is presumed to be a Kosovar citizen until proven otherwise.
  • A child born in Kosovo who would otherwise be stateless acquires Kosovar citizenship at birth.
  • Kosovo’s citizenship law explicitly addresses statelessness prevention in accordance with international standards, though Kosovo is not a party to the 1954 or 1961 UN conventions on statelessness (as it is not a UN member state).
  • The Roma, Ashkali, and Egyptian communities in Kosovo are disproportionately affected by statelessness and documentation gaps, a legacy of displacement during the 1998—1999 conflict. UNHCR and civil society organizations have worked to address these gaps through civil registration campaigns.

Document Checklist

  • Completed citizenship application form (from the Ministry of Internal Affairs or Kosovar embassy)
  • Valid passport or travel document (or proof of identity if stateless)
  • Birth certificate (apostilled/legalized, with certified Albanian or Serbian translation)
  • Marriage certificate (if applying via spousal pathway)
  • Proof of 5 years continuous lawful residence in Kosovo (or 3 years for spousal pathway)
  • Criminal background check from Kosovo and countries of prior residence
  • Proof of lawful income or financial means
  • Proof of language knowledge (Albanian or Serbian)
  • Two biometric passport photos
  • Receipt of EUR 50 application fee
