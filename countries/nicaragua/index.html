Dual Citizenship

Yes — Nicaragua has permitted dual (and multiple) nationality since the 1995 constitutional reform. Nicaraguan nationals who acquire another citizenship do not lose their Nicaraguan nationality.

Prior to the 1995 reform, acquiring a foreign citizenship could result in loss of Nicaraguan nationality. The reform to Article 20 of the Constitution explicitly established that Nicaraguan nationality is not lost by acquiring another nationality. This was a regional trend — several Central American nations adopted similar provisions in the 1990s to protect the rights of large diaspora populations, particularly in the United States.

Key point: Unlike some countries that distinguish between nationals by birth and naturalized citizens regarding dual nationality, Nicaragua’s constitutional provision is broad. However, naturalized Nicaraguans who voluntarily acquire another nationality after naturalization may face additional scrutiny, and the State reserves the right to revoke naturalization in cases of fraud or prolonged absence.

Acquisition of Nationality

By Birth (Jus Soli)

Nicaragua applies unrestricted jus soli. Any person born on Nicaraguan territory acquires Nicaraguan nationality by birth, regardless of the nationality of their parents.

Constitutional basis: Article 16 of the Political Constitution of Nicaragua.

This includes:

  • Children born anywhere within Nicaraguan national territory (including territorial waters, the Caribbean coast, and airspace)
  • Children born on Nicaraguan-flagged vessels or aircraft
  • Foundlings (children of unknown parentage found on Nicaraguan territory are presumed Nicaraguan)

By Descent (Jus Sanguinis)

Nicaraguan nationality by birth also extends to children born abroad to Nicaraguan parents:

ScenarioRequirementConstitutional Basis
Born abroad to a Nicaraguan father or motherAt least one parent is Nicaraguan nationalArt. 16 of the Constitution
Born abroad — minor childParent must register the child at a Nicaraguan consulate or upon return to NicaraguaArt. 16; Ley 149
Born abroad — adult childMay claim nationality by filing declaration and providing proof of parentageArt. 16; Ley 149

Registration requirement: Children born abroad to Nicaraguan parents should be registered at a Nicaraguan consulate. While there is no strict deadline, registration is necessary to exercise nationality rights (obtain a cedula, passport, vote). Adults who discover they have a claim to Nicaraguan nationality by descent can file at any age.

Generational limits: The Constitution does not impose an explicit generational limit. However, in practice, the applicant must demonstrate a direct chain of Nicaraguan parentage through documentary evidence (birth certificates, parents’ cedulas or passports).

By Adoption

Adoption does not automatically confer Nicaraguan nationality upon the adopted child. However:

  • A foreign child adopted by Nicaraguan nationals may apply for naturalization with facilitated procedures
  • Minor children adopted by Nicaraguans and residing in Nicaragua may be included in a simplified naturalization process
  • The adopted child retains the right to choose Nicaraguan nationality upon reaching the age of majority (18 years)

By Marriage

Marriage to a Nicaraguan national does not automatically confer nationality. However, it significantly reduces the residency requirement for naturalization:

RequirementDetail
Residency1 year of legal residence in Nicaragua (reduced from 4 years)
Marriage statusMust be legally married to a Nicaraguan national at time of application
CohabitationMust demonstrate conjugal life in Nicaragua during the residency period
LanguageSpanish proficiency evaluated during interview (no formal exam)
Good conductClean criminal record required
RenunciationMust formally renounce previous nationality before Nicaraguan authorities (procedural, not substantive — does not affect status in the other country)

Note: The renunciation requirement is a formality under Nicaraguan law. Since Nicaragua recognizes dual nationality, this declaration does not actually cause loss of the other nationality in practice, and the other country’s laws determine whether nationality is actually lost.

By Naturalization (Residency)

Foreign nationals who have resided legally in Nicaragua may apply for naturalization (carta de naturalizacion) through the National Assembly, which holds constitutional authority over naturalization grants.

Preferred Pathways

Nicaragua extends preferential treatment to nationals of Central American and Ibero-American countries, reflecting deep historical, cultural, and linguistic ties — particularly within the CA-4 free movement zone.

CA-4 Free Movement Agreement

Nicaragua is a founding member of the CA-4 (Central America-4) Free Movement Agreement, established in 2006, which allows citizens of the four member states to travel freely across borders without passports or visas, using only a national identity card (cedula).

CA-4 practical details:

  • Citizens of CA-4 countries can cross borders using only their national identity document (no passport required)
  • Free movement applies to tourism, transit, and short-term stays (up to 90 days shared across the CA-4 zone)
  • Third-country nationals who enter one CA-4 country receive a single 90-day period valid across all four nations
  • The agreement facilitates labor mobility and regional integration, though work permits are still technically required for employment
  • Residents of CA-4 countries benefit from reduced naturalization requirements in Nicaragua (2 years instead of 4)

Special Clauses & Programs

Honorary Nationality

The National Assembly of Nicaragua may grant Nicaraguan nationality by special resolution to foreign nationals who have rendered exceptional services to the nation. This is an honorary designation and does not require the standard residency or procedural requirements.

Central American Union Provisions

Nicaragua’s Constitution contains provisions reflecting the aspiration of Central American political union, a legacy of the Federal Republic of Central America (1823-1841). Article 18 states that Central Americans have the right to opt for Nicaraguan nationality, consistent with the principle of a unified Central American identity. This provision, while largely aspirational, underpins the preferential treatment of Central American nationals in naturalization.

Nationality for Refugees and Stateless Persons

Nicaragua is a party to the 1951 Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees and the 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness. Refugees and stateless persons may apply for naturalization with reduced requirements, typically at 2 years of residency, subject to the discretion of immigration authorities.

No Formal Citizenship by Investment Program

Nicaragua does not offer a formal citizenship-by-investment (CBI) or “golden passport” program. There is no direct path from investment to nationality. All naturalization requires physical residency and completion of the standard procedural requirements.

Political Context

Nicaragua’s political environment under President Daniel Ortega (in power since 2007, with earlier tenure from 1979-1990) has significant implications for nationality and immigration matters. Since the 2018 political crisis:

  • The government has revoked the nationality of prominent opposition figures and critics, including over 300 individuals stripped of citizenship by legislative decree in 2023-2024
  • International sanctions from the United States, the European Union, and Canada affect banking, investment, and travel
  • Some foreign nationals have been expelled or denied entry on political grounds
  • The legal environment for foreigners can be unpredictable, and immigration decisions may be influenced by political considerations
  • Despite this, the naturalization process remains available for applicants who meet standard requirements

Prospective applicants should be aware that the political situation may affect processing times, bureaucratic responsiveness, and the practical exercise of rights.

Investment Pathways

Citizenship by Investment (CBI)

Not available. Nicaragua does not offer a direct path from investment to citizenship. All naturalization requires physical residency.

Residency by Investment / Pensionado Programs

Nicaragua offers several residency categories that can serve as a pathway to eventual naturalization:

Path to citizenship: After obtaining legal residency, the applicant must accumulate 4 years of continuous legal residence (or 2 years for Ibero-American/Central American nationals, or 1 year if married to a Nicaraguan) before applying for naturalization.

Pensionado program advantages: Nicaragua’s retiree residency program is one of the most affordable in the Western Hemisphere. The $600/month income threshold is significantly lower than Costa Rica ($1,000/month), Panama ($1,000/month), or Mexico (~$2,600/month). Benefits include duty-free import of household goods (up to $20,000), a vehicle (every 5 years), and exemption from income tax on foreign-sourced retirement income.

Business & Entrepreneurship

Nicaragua is the second-poorest country in the Western Hemisphere (after Haiti by GDP per capita), which creates both challenges and opportunities. Extremely low operating costs make it attractive for certain business models, particularly in textiles, agriculture, and light manufacturing for export. However, the political environment, international sanctions, weak institutions, and limited infrastructure temper the business climate significantly.

Company Formation

Most common entity typeSociedad Anonima (S.A.) — Corporation
Formation timeline2-4 weeks
Formation cost~$500-$1,500 USD (including notary, registration, and legal fees)
Minimum capitalC$10,000 (~$275 USD) — Nicaraguan Cordobas
Online registrationNot available — in-person notarization and registration required

The Sociedad Anonima (S.A.) is the standard corporate vehicle for businesses in Nicaragua. Formation requires:

  • Drafting articles of incorporation (escritura de constitucion) before a notary public
  • Registration with the Registro Publico Mercantil (Commercial Registry)
  • Registration with the Direccion General de Ingresos (DGI — tax authority) for a RUC number (tax ID)
  • Registration with the local municipality (Alcaldia) for a municipal operating license
  • Registration with INSS (social security) if hiring employees

Foreign nationals can own 100% of a Nicaraguan company. There are no restrictions on foreign ownership in most sectors, though certain strategic industries (mining, fisheries, forestry) may require government concessions.

Sole proprietorship (Persona Natural con Negocio) is simpler but offers no liability protection. The Sociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada (S.R.L.) — limited liability company — is available but less commonly used than the S.A.

Entrepreneur & Startup Visas

Nicaragua does not have a dedicated startup or entrepreneur visa. Common pathways:

  • Temporary Resident Visa (Residente Temporal): Allows business activities; requires proof of economic solvency or business investment. 1-2 year duration, renewable
  • Investor Residency: For those establishing or investing in a Nicaraguan business ($30,000+ investment). Leads to temporary residency
  • Permanent Resident (Residente Permanente): Available after 2-4 years of temporary residency, or immediately under the Pensionado/Rentista programs. Unrestricted work rights
  • Business Visa (Visa de Negocios): 30-90 day single/multiple entry visa for business meetings and negotiations (not for employment or long-term stay)

Free Trade Zones (Zonas Francas): Nicaragua’s Zona Franca regime, governed by Ley 917 (Ley General de Zonas Francas de Exportacion), is the country’s most significant tax incentive. Qualifying enterprises within designated free trade zones receive:

  • 100% exemption from corporate income tax for up to 10 years (renewable for additional periods)
  • 100% exemption from import duties on raw materials, equipment, and machinery
  • 100% exemption from export taxes
  • 100% exemption from municipal taxes
  • 100% exemption from VAT on local purchases of goods and services used in production
  • Exemption from capital gains tax on the sale of assets within the zone

The Zona Franca system has been the backbone of Nicaragua’s textile/apparel manufacturing sector (maquila), attracting investment from companies producing for the United States market under CAFTA-DR (Central America-Dominican Republic Free Trade Agreement).

CAFTA-DR: Nicaragua is a member of CAFTA-DR, which provides preferential tariff access to the US market for qualifying goods, particularly textiles, apparel, and agricultural products.

Minimum wage context: Nicaragua has one of the lowest minimum wages in the hemisphere (approximately $200-$250/month depending on sector), making it competitive for labor-intensive manufacturing.

Banking & Financial Infrastructure

  • Major banks: Banpro (Grupo Promerica, largest), BAC Credomatic, Banco Lafise Bancentro, Banco de la Produccion (Banpro), Banco Ficohsa
  • International sanctions impact: Due to US and EU sanctions targeting Nicaraguan government officials and entities, some international banking relationships have been strained. Wire transfers to/from Nicaragua may face additional compliance scrutiny
  • Stripe is not available in Nicaragua
  • SINPE — Nicaragua’s interbank payment system (less developed than regional peers)
  • Foreign currency accounts are available (USD accounts common at major banks)
  • RUC (tax ID) required to open any business bank account; foreigners need residency card
  • Correspondent banking relationships have diminished since 2018; some international transfers face delays or rejection
  • Cryptocurrency is not regulated but not formally recognized as legal tender

Practical banking note: Due to the sanctions environment, foreigners establishing businesses in Nicaragua should verify that their home country banks will process transfers to Nicaraguan institutions before committing to investment.

Key Programs & Incentives

  • Zonas Francas (Free Trade Zones) — The primary investment incentive (see Tax Overview above). Over 200 companies operate in Nicaraguan free trade zones, employing approximately 120,000 workers
  • CAFTA-DR — Preferential trade access to the US market for qualifying goods
  • ProNicaragua — The official investment promotion agency (though effectiveness has been affected by political changes)
  • Ley de Concertacion Tributaria (Ley 822) — The main tax code, which includes incentives for agriculture, tourism, and renewable energy
  • Tourism Incentives (Ley 306) — Tax exemptions for qualifying tourism projects: 80-100% income tax exemption for up to 10 years, duty-free import of construction materials and equipment, property tax exemptions
  • Renewable Energy Incentives (Ley 532) — Tax benefits for renewable energy projects, including hydroelectric, geothermal, solar, and wind
  • Forestry Law incentives — Tax exemptions for reforestation and sustainable forestry projects
  • Key sectors: Textiles/apparel (largest Zona Franca sector), agriculture (coffee, beef, sugar, peanuts, tobacco), light manufacturing, call centers/BPO, tourism, mining (gold)

Loss of Nationality

Voluntary Renunciation

  • Nationals by birth: May voluntarily renounce Nicaraguan nationality by formal declaration. However, since the 1995 reform, acquiring another nationality does not automatically trigger loss.
  • Naturalized citizens: May voluntarily renounce by formal declaration before competent authorities.

Involuntary Loss

Nationals by birth generally cannot lose their Nicaraguan nationality involuntarily under normal circumstances. The 1995 constitutional reform strengthened protections against involuntary loss. However:

  • Political revocations (2023-2024): The Nicaraguan government, through National Assembly decrees, has stripped nationality from over 300 individuals deemed to be opponents of the state, including journalists, human rights defenders, clergy, and former political figures. These actions have been widely condemned by international organizations as violations of international law, which prohibits rendering individuals stateless. The legality of these revocations under Nicaragua’s own Constitution is disputed.

Naturalized citizens may lose their Nicaraguan nationality in the following cases:

CauseDetail
FraudIf naturalization was obtained through false information, fraudulent documents, or concealment of material facts
Extended absenceProlonged absence from Nicaragua without justification (typically 3+ years) may be grounds for revocation
Crimes against the StateConviction of serious crimes against national security or sovereignty
Voluntary acquisition of another nationality (pre-1995)No longer applicable since the 1995 reform
Service to a foreign government without authorizationActing as an agent of a foreign state against Nicaraguan interests

Practical Guide

Documents Needed

For naturalization by residency (standard checklist):

  • Completed naturalization application form
  • Valid passport (original + certified copy)
  • Current Nicaraguan residency card (cedula de residencia) — original + copy
  • Birth certificate (original, apostilled or legalized, translated into Spanish by a certified translator)
  • Criminal background check from country of origin (apostilled, translated into Spanish)
  • Criminal background check from Nicaragua (issued by the Policia Nacional)
  • Medical certificate from a Nicaraguan-authorized physician
  • Two recent passport-sized photographs (white background)
  • Proof of legal entry into Nicaragua (entry stamps, immigration records)
  • Proof of continuous residency (utility bills, lease agreements, bank statements, employment records)
  • Proof of financial solvency (bank statements, employment letter, business registration)
  • Marriage certificate (if applying under marriage category — apostilled, translated)
  • Spouse’s Nicaraguan cedula or nationality documentation (if applying under marriage category)
  • Written declaration of renunciation of previous nationality (procedural — does not affect other country’s laws)
  • Payment receipt for application fees

For registration of nationality by descent (born abroad):

  • Birth certificate of the applicant (apostilled, translated)
  • Birth certificate or cedula of the Nicaraguan parent(s)
  • Parent’s Nicaraguan passport or proof of Nicaraguan nationality
  • Two recent passport-sized photographs
  • Application form
