Dual Citizenship — Conditional

Israel’s approach to dual citizenship depends entirely on how citizenship was acquired. Immigrants who obtain citizenship under the Law of Return (1950) may retain their prior nationality — Israel does not require them to renounce. However, persons who obtain Israeli citizenship through naturalization (residency-based) are required to renounce their prior nationality as a condition of approval. In practice, Israel tolerates dual citizenship widely because of the dominant role of the Law of Return, and millions of Israeli citizens hold second passports. Israeli-born citizens who acquire a foreign nationality do not lose Israeli citizenship.

Acquisition of Nationality

Israeli nationality law is governed by two foundational statutes:

  1. Law of Return, 5710-1950 (amended 1954, 1970) — Establishes the right of every Jewish person to immigrate to Israel (make Aliyah) and receive citizenship.
  2. Nationality Law, 5712-1952 (amended multiple times) — Governs citizenship by birth, residency, naturalization, and other means.

These two laws operate in parallel but address different populations. The Law of Return is Israel’s defining citizenship mechanism and has no equivalent in any other country’s nationality law.

By Birth (Jus Soli) — Very Limited

Israel is not a jus soli country. Birth on Israeli territory does not confer citizenship except in narrow circumstances:

  • Born in Israel to an Israeli citizen parent: The child is an Israeli citizen from birth (Section 4, Nationality Law). This is technically jus sanguinis applied within the territory.
  • Born in Israel to stateless parents or parents of unknown nationality: The child may acquire Israeli citizenship to prevent statelessness, subject to conditions.
  • Foundlings: A child found in Israel whose parents are unknown is presumed to be an Israeli citizen until proven otherwise.
  • Born in Israel to non-citizen parents: No citizenship is conferred. The child takes the nationality of the parents.

By Descent (Jus Sanguinis)

Israeli citizenship passes by descent in the following ways:

Through the Nationality Law (Section 4)

  • A child born to an Israeli citizen parent (father or mother) acquires Israeli citizenship at birth, regardless of where the child is born (in Israel or abroad).
  • This applies automatically — no application or registration is required for the first generation born abroad.
  • Second generation born abroad: A child born outside Israel to an Israeli parent who was also born outside Israel may acquire citizenship, but registration is required and the connection to Israel may be examined. In practice, citizenship is generally recognized if the parent holds or held Israeli citizenship.
  • There is no strict generational cutoff in law, but administrative practice becomes more complex for successive generations born abroad without maintaining ties to Israel.

Through the Law of Return (Functional Descent)

  • Even if a person is not born to an Israeli citizen, they may claim Israeli citizenship based on Jewish descent (see Law of Return section below). This functions as a form of ethno-national jus sanguinis with no generational limit, as long as the applicant can demonstrate Jewish heritage within the scope of the law (up to a grandchild of a Jewish person).

By Adoption

  • A minor adopted by an Israeli citizen may acquire Israeli citizenship. The process depends on whether the adoption took place in Israel or abroad.
  • If the adoption is recognized under Israeli law and the adoptive parent is an Israeli citizen, the child is generally eligible for citizenship.
  • There is no automatic grant — an application must be made to the Ministry of Interior.
  • The child’s prior nationality is not affected by the grant of Israeli citizenship (Israel does not require the child to renounce).

By Marriage

Marriage to an Israeli citizen does not automatically confer Israeli citizenship. However, a foreign spouse of an Israeli citizen may apply for citizenship through a graduated status process administered by the Ministry of Interior:

  1. Initial temporary residency permit (A/5 visa): Granted upon application, typically valid for 1 year.
  2. Renewals: The A/5 visa is renewed annually, usually for a total of 4-5 years.
  3. Permanent residency or citizenship: After approximately 4-5 years of continuous residence in Israel on an A/5 visa, the spouse may apply for permanent residency or Israeli citizenship.

Key Conditions

  • The couple must demonstrate a genuine marriage and center of life in Israel. The Ministry of Interior actively investigates sham marriages.
  • If the Israeli spouse is an Arab citizen of Israel and the foreign spouse is a Palestinian from the West Bank or Gaza, the process is subject to additional restrictions under the Citizenship and Entry into Israel Law (Temporary Provision), 2003, which limits family reunification in these cases.
  • The foreign spouse must renounce prior nationality if granted citizenship through naturalization (as opposed to under the Law of Return).
  • If the Israeli citizen spouse dies during the process, the foreign spouse may continue the application under humanitarian provisions.

By Naturalization (Residency)

Foreign nationals who are not eligible under the Law of Return may apply for Israeli citizenship by naturalization under Section 5 of the Nationality Law:

RequirementDetails
ResidencyPresent in Israel for 3 of the preceding 5 years before application
Right to resideMust hold a valid residency permit (permanent residency or appropriate visa)
Settlement intentionMust intend to settle permanently in Israel
LanguageSome knowledge of Hebrew (no standardized test; assessed informally)
RenunciationMust renounce prior nationality, or demonstrate that they will cease to be a foreign national upon acquiring Israeli citizenship
CharacterGood character (no serious criminal record)
Ministerial discretionCitizenship by naturalization is granted at the discretion of the Minister of Interior — it is not an entitlement

Important: Unlike the Law of Return, which creates a right to citizenship, naturalization under Section 5 is entirely discretionary. The Minister of Interior may refuse an application even if all formal criteria are met. In practice, naturalization is uncommon and is most often used by foreign spouses who are not eligible under the Law of Return.

Exemptions by Ministerial or Governmental Discretion

  • The Minister of Interior may waive some or all requirements for naturalization in special cases.
  • Persons who have performed military service in the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) are eligible for expedited citizenship.
  • Foreign nationals who have made significant contributions to Israel may receive citizenship by special resolution of the government.

Where Your Passport Opens Doors

An Israeli passport provides:

  • Visa-free or visa-on-arrival: 160+ countries and territories, including all of Europe (Schengen Area), United Kingdom, Canada (eTA), Brazil, Japan, South Korea, Australia (ETA), and Mexico.
  • United States: Israelis may apply for B-1/B-2 visas; Israel has been working toward joining the US Visa Waiver Program but as of 2025 had not yet been admitted.
  • Notable restrictions: An Israeli passport is not accepted for entry in several countries, including Iran, Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Libya, Yemen, and others that do not recognize the State of Israel. Some countries (Malaysia, Bangladesh, Brunei, Pakistan) officially bar entry to Israeli passport holders, though enforcement varies.
  • Second passport strategy: Many Israeli citizens hold a second passport (often European — German, Polish, Romanian, French, Portuguese, or Spanish) specifically to travel to countries that do not accept Israeli documents.
  • Consular protection: Israeli diplomatic missions provide robust consular services worldwide, though the network is smaller than that of large European nations.

Special Clauses & Programs

The Law of Return — Israel’s Defining Feature

The Law of Return (Chok HaShvut), 5710-1950 is the single most important piece of Israeli nationality legislation. It has no parallel in any other country’s legal system.

Who Is Eligible

Under the Law of Return as amended in 1970 (Amendment 2):

  1. Any Jewish person — A person born to a Jewish mother (matrilineal descent per halakha) or who has undergone a recognized conversion to Judaism.
  2. A child of a Jewish person — Regardless of whether the child is themselves Jewish under halakha.
  3. A grandchild of a Jewish person — The so-called “grandchild clause” of Amendment 2. This was deliberately designed to encompass anyone who would have been persecuted as Jewish under the Nuremberg Laws.
  4. The spouse of any of the above — Including the spouse of a child or grandchild of a Jewish person, even if the spouse has no Jewish ancestry whatsoever.

Who Is Excluded

The Law of Return excludes:

  • A person who has been Jewish and voluntarily converted to another religion (though administrative application varies).
  • A person who is a danger to the welfare of the state.
  • A person who is a danger to public health or state security.
  • A person with a serious criminal past likely to endanger public welfare.

Note: The “who is a Jew” question is one of the most contentious political and religious issues in Israel. The Orthodox rabbinate’s definition (matrilineal descent or Orthodox conversion) sometimes conflicts with the Law of Return’s broader administrative application, which accepts non-Orthodox conversions for immigration purposes. This creates situations where a person may be eligible for citizenship under the Law of Return but not recognized as Jewish for personal status matters (marriage, burial, etc.) administered by the Rabbinate.

The Aliyah Process

Aliyah (literally “ascent”) is the process of Jewish immigration to Israel under the Law of Return:

  1. Contact the Jewish Agency for Israel (Sochnut): The Jewish Agency is the worldwide organization that facilitates Aliyah. Website: jewishagency.org. They operate Aliyah offices in most countries with significant Jewish populations.
  2. Submit documentation: Proof of Jewish identity (typically a letter from a recognized rabbi, Jewish community records, a parent’s or grandparent’s documents showing Jewish identity, or evidence of recognized conversion). Additional documents include passport, birth certificate, criminal background check, passport photos, and medical forms.
  3. Interview: An emissary (shaliach) from the Jewish Agency or the Israeli consulate interviews the applicant to verify eligibility.
  4. Aliyah visa: Upon approval, the applicant receives an Aliyah visa (Visa Alef-1) at the Israeli consulate. Alternatively, applicants may arrive in Israel as tourists and begin the process at the Ministry of Interior.
  5. Arrival in Israel: Upon arrival (or upon approval in Israel), the new immigrant receives an Oleh Hadash (new immigrant) certificate and a Teudat Zehut (Israeli identity card).
  6. Citizenship: Citizenship under the Law of Return is granted automatically upon immigration unless the immigrant explicitly declines it. The immigrant may request permanent residency instead of citizenship.

Ulpan — Hebrew Language Integration

Every new immigrant is entitled to attend Ulpan, an intensive Hebrew language program:

  • Duration: Approximately 5 months (500 hours) of instruction.
  • Cost: Free for new immigrants (funded by the Ministry of Aliyah and Absorption).
  • Schedule: Morning or evening classes available, designed to accommodate working immigrants.
  • Levels: From absolute beginner (Alef) through advanced.
  • Locations: Ulpan schools exist in every major Israeli city and many smaller communities.
  • Kibbutz Ulpan: An alternative format where immigrants live on a kibbutz, combining Hebrew study with work on the kibbutz. Room and board provided.

Nefesh B’Nefesh — North American Aliyah

Nefesh B’Nefesh is a nonprofit organization that partners with the Jewish Agency and the Israeli government to facilitate Aliyah specifically from North America (United States and Canada) and the United Kingdom:

  • Provides personalized guidance through the Aliyah application process
  • Additional financial grants beyond the government Sal Klita
  • Employment assistance and professional licensing guidance
  • Community integration support
  • Charter flights for groups of new immigrants (often with media coverage)
  • Website: nbn.org.il

Birthright Israel (Taglit)

While not a citizenship program, Birthright Israel provides free 10-day educational trips to Israel for Jewish young adults (ages 18-32) who have not previously visited on a peer trip. Many Birthright participants later make Aliyah. The program has brought over 800,000 participants since its founding in 1999.

Section 4A — Arab Residents and the Nationality Law

Arab residents of Israel who were present in the territory at the time of the state’s founding (May 14, 1948) could acquire citizenship under Section 3 of the Nationality Law if they met certain residency conditions. Their descendants acquire citizenship by birth (Section 4). This pathway is historically significant but no longer applicable to new applicants.

Investment Pathways

Citizenship by Investment (CBI)

Israel does not offer a citizenship-by-investment program. There is no investment amount that leads directly to Israeli citizenship. Citizenship is available through the Law of Return, birth/descent, or naturalization — none of which are investment-based.

Golden Visa / Residency by Investment (RBI)

Israel does not have a formal golden visa or residency-by-investment program comparable to those offered by European countries. Residency is obtained through:

  • Law of Return (immediate permanent status for eligible persons)
  • Work visas (B/1) — employer-sponsored
  • Expert visas — for foreign professionals with specialized knowledge
  • Investor/business visas: While there is no formal RBI program, foreign nationals who establish businesses or make significant investments in Israel may apply for B/1 work visas or temporary residency permits. These are evaluated on a case-by-case basis and do not follow a standardized investment threshold.
  • Startup/Innovation Visa (pilot programs): Israel has piloted programs to attract foreign entrepreneurs to its startup ecosystem, but these have not been formalized into permanent law as of this writing.

Bottom line: For persons eligible under the Law of Return, investment programs are irrelevant — citizenship is immediate and free. For non-Jewish persons, there is no investment shortcut to Israeli citizenship.

Business & Entrepreneurship

Israel is the original “Start-Up Nation” — with more startups per capita than any other country, the highest venture capital investment per capita globally, and a culture that celebrates entrepreneurial risk-taking. The mandatory military service, particularly in elite technology units like 8200, creates a pipeline of technically skilled founders.

Israel’s most common startup entity is the Private Limited Company (Ltd.). Foreign nationals can own 100% of an Israeli company with no restrictions. Registration is done through the Registrar of Companies. Other structures include partnerships, sole proprietorships, and public companies (Ltd. Publicit).

Entrepreneur & Startup Visas

Israel does not have a dedicated “startup visa” in the traditional sense. Key pathways for foreign entrepreneurs:

  • B/1 Expert Visa: For foreign professionals invited to work at Israeli companies, commonly used by startup founders with Israeli co-founders or investors
  • Innovation Visa (Pilot Program): Launched periodically for foreign entrepreneurs developing innovative technology in Israel
  • A/1 Temporary Residence: Available to those with substantial business ties to Israel
  • Aliyah (Law of Return): Jewish individuals worldwide can immigrate and receive citizenship, the most common pathway for foreign founders in Israeli startups

The lack of a formal startup visa is partially offset by the strength of the Law of Return for eligible individuals and the active involvement of Israeli VCs who sponsor expert visas.

Loss of Nationality

Voluntary Renunciation

An Israeli citizen may renounce Israeli citizenship (Section 10, Nationality Law):

  • The person must be residing outside Israel at the time of renunciation.
  • The person must not thereby become stateless (must hold or be entitled to another nationality).
  • The renunciation must be approved by the Minister of Interior, who has discretion to refuse.
  • Men of military age: Males who have not completed their military service obligations or received an exemption may face additional hurdles in renunciation. In practice, the Ministry may delay approval until military obligations are resolved.
  • Tax clearance: The Israel Tax Authority may require settlement of outstanding tax obligations before renunciation is processed.

Involuntary Loss

Israeli citizenship may be revoked in limited circumstances:

  • Fraud: Citizenship obtained through false information may be revoked (Section 11).
  • Disloyalty or treason: An Israeli citizen who commits an act of disloyalty to the State (broadly defined, including acquiring citizenship or permanent residency in an enemy state — Iran, Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, as listed) may have citizenship revoked (Section 11(b)). This provision has been applied rarely but remains on the books.
  • 2008 Amendment — Terrorism: The Nationality Law was amended to allow revocation of citizenship for persons convicted of terrorism, espionage, or treason, or those who acquired citizenship or residency in an enemy state. The Minister of Interior may revoke citizenship with the approval of a district court.
  • Living in an enemy state: An Israeli citizen who resides in or acquires nationality of a state listed as an enemy state (under the Prevention of Infiltration Law and related legislation) may lose citizenship.
  • Naturalized citizens: Citizens who acquired citizenship by naturalization (Section 5) rather than under the Law of Return face a slightly lower threshold for revocation.

Recovery / Resumption

  • There is no general statutory right to recover Israeli citizenship once renounced or revoked.
  • A former citizen may apply for citizenship through any applicable pathway (e.g., Law of Return if eligible, or naturalization).
  • In practice, former citizens who are eligible under the Law of Return can simply make Aliyah again and re-acquire citizenship.
  • The Minister of Interior has discretion to restore citizenship in individual cases.

Practical Guide

Documents Required

For Aliyah (Law of Return)

  • Proof of Jewish identity (rabbi’s letter, community records, parent/grandparent documents, or conversion certificate)
  • Birth certificate (apostilled)
  • Passport (valid for at least 6 months beyond planned arrival)
  • Passport-sized photos (white background, as specified by consulate)
  • Police clearance certificate from country of residence (and any country lived in for 6+ months as an adult)
  • Marriage certificate (if applicable, especially if spouse is applying based on the applicant’s eligibility)
  • Death certificate of Jewish parent/grandparent (if the linking relative is deceased)
  • Parent’s or grandparent’s birth certificate showing Jewish identity (if claiming through descent)
  • Military service records (if applicable)
  • Medical forms (specific forms provided by the Jewish Agency; tuberculosis screening required)
  • CV/resume (requested by some consulates for planning absorption services)
  • Proof of education (university degrees, professional certifications — for licensing in Israel)

For Naturalization (Section 5)

  • Valid residency permit (temporary or permanent residency documentation)
  • Proof of 3 years’ presence in Israel within the preceding 5 years (entry/exit stamps, rent receipts, employment records)
  • Hebrew language demonstration (no formal certificate required; assessed during interview)
  • Renunciation of prior nationality (or evidence of willingness to renounce)
  • Police clearance certificate
  • Passport and identity documents
  • Application fee: NIS 690
  • Passport-sized photos

Tips

  • Document your Jewish lineage carefully. The single most important step for Law of Return applicants is assembling clear documentation of Jewish ancestry. The Jewish Agency reviews these documents closely. Gather everything: grandparents’ ketubah (marriage contract), synagogue membership records, cemetery records, community organization documents, Holocaust restitution records, or any official document identifying a family member as Jewish.
  • Consider applying from within Israel. Tourists who are eligible under the Law of Return can visit Israel, begin the process at the Ministry of Interior, and receive citizenship status quickly — sometimes within days.
  • Start Ulpan early. Hebrew fluency is essential for integration. Even starting Hebrew studies before Aliyah (via online programs or community classes) will ease the transition significantly.
  • Understand the tax implications. Israel offers a 10-year exemption on reporting and taxation of foreign-sourced income for new immigrants. This is one of the most generous tax benefit packages for immigrants anywhere in the world. Consult a tax advisor familiar with both Israeli law and your home country’s tax obligations.
  • Military service applies. Male citizens under 26 and female citizens under 22 (approximate age thresholds, subject to change) who make Aliyah are generally subject to IDF military service. Exemptions and reduced service terms apply depending on age at Aliyah, marital status, and other factors.
  • Non-Jewish spouses should prepare for a long process. The spousal graduated status process (A/5 visa renewals over 4-5+ years) is administratively burdensome and requires annual renewals, interviews, and proof of a genuine center of life in Israel. Engage an immigration attorney experienced in Israeli law.
