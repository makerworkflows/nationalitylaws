Dual Citizenship

Malaysia does not permit dual citizenship. This prohibition is rooted in the Federal Constitution and has been consistently upheld by the courts.

Article 24: A Malaysian citizen who voluntarily acquires citizenship of another country ceases to be a citizen of Malaysia. The acquisition need not be formalized through a renunciation process — the loss is automatic by constitutional operation.

Article 24(2): If the government is satisfied that a citizen has acquired foreign citizenship, the government shall revoke the person’s Malaysian citizenship.

Consequences:

  • Malaysian citizens who obtain foreign passports or take oaths of allegiance to foreign states lose their Malaysian citizenship.
  • There are no exceptions for any category of person.
  • The government actively monitors and enforces this prohibition, particularly through passport and identity card databases.
  • Malaysian children born abroad in jus soli countries (e.g., the United States) who hold both nationalities must choose before turning 22 or face automatic loss of Malaysian citizenship.

Acquisition of Nationality

By Birth (Jus Soli)

Malaysia’s jus soli is conditional and has been narrowed over time.

Article 14(1)(b) and the Second Schedule:

  • A person born in Malaysia is a citizen by operation of law if at least one parent is a citizen or permanent resident of Malaysia at the time of birth.
  • A person born in Malaysia is not automatically a citizen if both parents are foreign nationals, even if the parents are lawfully present.

Pre-1962 births: Persons born in the Federation before Malaysia Day (16 September 1963) have separate provisions under the original 1957 Constitution.

By Descent (Jus Sanguinis)

Citizenship by descent has been a contentious issue in Malaysia, particularly regarding gender equality:

Father’s descent: A person born abroad whose father is a Malaysian citizen is a citizen by descent (Article 14, Second Schedule, Section 1(b)).

Mother’s descent: Historically, Malaysian women could not independently transmit citizenship to children born abroad. This was challenged in the landmark Suriani Kempe case and subsequent litigation. As of 2024—2025 constitutional amendments, Malaysia has moved toward allowing mothers to transmit citizenship to children born overseas, though implementation details remain subject to gazetted regulations.

Registration requirement: Children born abroad to Malaysian parents must be registered with the nearest Malaysian High Commission or Embassy.

By Adoption

Adoption does not automatically confer Malaysian citizenship. Adopted children of Malaysian citizens may apply for citizenship through registration under Article 15A of the Constitution, which gives the Federal Government discretion to register any person under 21 as a citizen.

In practice, this is a difficult and lengthy process, and many adoption cases are denied or delayed for years.

By Marriage

There is no automatic acquisition of citizenship through marriage. A foreign wife of a Malaysian citizen may apply for citizenship by registration under Article 15(1):

  1. Must be married to a Malaysian citizen.
  2. Must have resided in Malaysia for at least 2 years immediately preceding the application.
  3. Must intend to reside permanently in Malaysia.
  4. Must be of good character.
  5. Must have adequate knowledge of Malay.

Gender disparity: This provision explicitly applies to “any woman who is married to a citizen.” Foreign husbands of Malaysian women do not have an equivalent registration pathway and must use the standard naturalization process (10 years residence). Reform efforts are ongoing.

Naturalization is discretionary. The government is under no obligation to grant citizenship even if all criteria are met. Processing times of 3—5 years or longer are common, and rejection rates are high.

Loss and Renunciation

Voluntary Renunciation

Article 23: A citizen of Malaysia who is of age and sound mind may renounce citizenship by registering a declaration. The renunciation takes effect upon registration. The government may delay registration during wartime.

Restoration of Nationality

There is no general statutory right to restoration of Malaysian citizenship. Former citizens may apply for re-naturalization under the standard Article 19 naturalization process, subject to 10 years of residence and all other requirements.

In exceptional cases, the Federal Government may exercise discretion to register a former citizen under special provisions, but this is rare and politically sensitive.

Stateless Persons and Foundlings

Malaysia is not a party to the 1954 or 1961 Statelessness Conventions.

  • Foundlings: Article 19B of the Constitution provides that a child found in Malaysia whose parents are unknown may be registered as a citizen. This is discretionary, not automatic.
  • Statelessness: Malaysia has a significant stateless population, including persons of Indian Tamil descent on former plantations, Bajau Laut communities in Sabah, and Filipino refugees in Sabah. Estimates range from 50,000 to several hundred thousand stateless persons.
  • The strict citizenship transmission rules (particularly the historical inability of mothers to transmit citizenship abroad) have contributed to individual statelessness cases.

Document Checklist

  • Completed Form H (application for naturalization) or applicable registration form
  • Birth certificate (original and certified copy)
  • Valid passport or travel document
  • Malaysian Identity Card (MyKad) of Malaysian spouse or parent (if applicable)
  • Marriage certificate (if applying through spousal registration)
  • Proof of 10 years residence (immigration stamps, employment records, tax returns)
  • Police clearance certificate (from Malaysia and country of origin)
  • Malay language proficiency evidence (interview or test)
  • Passport-sized photographs (4 copies, blue background)
  • Renunciation certificate of foreign nationality
  • Statutory declaration of intent to reside permanently in Malaysia
  • Character references from Malaysian citizens
