Dual Citizenship

Historical Context: The Prohibition and the 2012 Reversal

Haiti’s dual citizenship history is a story in two chapters.

Chapter 1 — The Prohibition (1987–2012): The 1987 Constitution, drafted in the wake of the fall of Jean-Claude Duvalier’s dictatorship, contained Article 15, which explicitly prohibited dual nationality. Any Haitian who voluntarily acquired the nationality of another state automatically lost Haitian nationality. This was framed as a measure to prevent the concentration of power by elites with foreign citizenship and to ensure that those governing Haiti had no loyalty to any other state.

This prohibition had severe consequences for the diaspora. Hundreds of thousands of Haitians living in the United States, Canada, and France who naturalized in their adopted countries technically forfeited their Haitian nationality, creating a legal and emotional rupture with their homeland.

Chapter 2 — The 2012 Amendment: On June 9, 2012, the Haitian parliament ratified a constitutional amendment that effectively removed the prohibition on dual nationality. The amendment deleted or modified the problematic Article 15 language, recognizing that:

  • The diaspora sends over $3 billion USD annually in remittances to Haiti — one of the country’s largest sources of income.
  • Restricting dual citizenship discouraged diaspora investment and political participation.
  • Countries with large diasporas generally benefit from permitting dual nationality.

Since 2012, Haitian nationals who acquire foreign citizenship do not automatically lose Haitian nationality, and persons who had lost Haitian nationality under the old rule may seek to resume it.

Current Status

As of 2026, dual citizenship is fully permitted under Haitian law. A Haitian national may hold the nationality of any other state without legal consequences to their Haitian status. Foreign nationals who naturalize as Haitian are not required to renounce their prior nationality.

However, certain public office restrictions remain. The Haitian Constitution, even as amended, contains provisions restricting the holding of certain sensitive positions (in the military, judiciary, or high government offices) to persons who hold only Haitian nationality or who have not held foreign nationality within a specified period. These are political restrictions, not a general prohibition on dual citizenship.

Acquisition of Nationality

Haitian nationality is governed by the Constitution of the Republic of Haiti (1987, as amended) and the Haitian Nationality Law (Décret sur la nationalité). The legal framework reflects Haiti’s unique position as the first Black republic in the world and the influence of both French Napoleonic law and the country’s revolutionary constitutional tradition.

By Birth (Jus Soli)

Haiti applies unrestricted jus soli. Any person born on Haitian territory acquires Haitian nationality at birth, regardless of the parents’ nationality or immigration status.

This strong jus soli rule reflects Haiti’s revolutionary constitutional tradition dating to the 1804 Declaration of Independence, which recognized all persons on Haitian soil as equal members of the nation. It is consistent with the historical practice of many Caribbean and Latin American states.

The traditional diplomatic exception (children of foreign diplomats in official service) applies by customary international law.

By Descent (Jus Sanguinis)

A person born outside Haiti acquires Haitian nationality by descent if:

  • At least one parent is a Haitian national at the time of birth.
  • The birth is registered at a Haitian consulate or through the Office National d’Identification (ONI) within the required timeframe.

Citizenship by descent is available in the first generation born abroad. Subsequent generations should register to establish the descent link formally.

Children born to Haitian parents who had previously lost their nationality under the 1987 prohibition (by naturalizing abroad) exist in a legally complex position — they may have a claim to Haitian nationality through descent once the parent’s status is restored under the 2012 amendment, but this requires case-by-case analysis.

By Adoption

A minor child legally adopted by a Haitian citizen may acquire Haitian nationality. The adoption must be recognized under Haitian law. Haiti’s adoption system has faced international scrutiny, particularly following the 2010 earthquake, but the legal pathway for nationality through adoption remains available for domestically recognized adoptions.

By Marriage

Marriage to a Haitian citizen does not automatically confer nationality. The spousal pathway provides:

  • 3 years of legal residence in Haiti after marriage to a Haitian citizen.
  • The marriage must be legally recognized in Haiti.
  • Subject to good character requirements.
  • Since the 2012 amendment, renunciation of prior nationality is no longer required.

By Naturalization (Residency)

Standard Requirements

  • 5 years of lawful and continuous residence in Haiti immediately before application.
  • Must be of legal age.
  • Must be of good character (police clearance from Haiti and country of origin).
  • Must demonstrate intention to remain domiciled in Haiti.
  • Must have renounced foreign nationality only if seeking certain public offices — not for general naturalization since 2012.
  • Basic proficiency in Haitian Creole and/or French is expected (both are official languages).
  • Application submitted to the Ministère de l’Intérieur et des Collectivités Territoriales (MICT).

Reduced Residency Categories

  • French-speaking nationals: Historically recognized through francophone ties; may benefit from reduced residency requirements in certain circumstances under administrative practice.
  • Spouses of Haitian nationals: 3 years (see Marriage section).
  • Persons of Haitian origin or descent: Simplified registration procedures may apply.
  • Persons from countries with special treaties: Haiti has some bilateral arrangements that affect residency requirements.

Preferred Pathways

For diaspora members: The 2012 amendment is the defining event. Haitians who naturalized in the United States, Canada, France, or elsewhere between 1987 and 2012 and technically lost their Haitian nationality can now seek to resume it. This is the most significant and most pursued pathway — reclaiming a nationality rather than acquiring a new one.

For persons of Haitian descent born abroad: Birth descent registration is the appropriate pathway. If parents were Haitian nationals at birth, the person can register their Haitian nationality through any Haitian consulate.

For foreigners: The 5-year naturalization track applies. Given Haiti’s political instability, establishing lawful and continuous residence for 5 years requires commitment. However, for those with genuine ties to Haiti — humanitarian workers, religious missionaries, diaspora spouses, or investors in reconstruction — citizenship is achievable.

For CARICOM nationals: Haiti is a full CARICOM member. CARICOM free movement rights apply in principle, though Haiti’s implementation of CARICOM protocols has been inconsistent due to institutional capacity constraints.

Special Clauses & Programs

First Black Republic

Haiti declared independence on January 1, 1804, following the only successful slave revolt in history. The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) was led by Jean-Jacques Dessalines, Toussaint Louverture, and Henri Christophe, and resulted in the defeat of Napoleonic France and the establishment of a sovereign Black republic at a time when slavery dominated the Americas.

This history is central to Haitian national identity and explains many features of Haitian nationality law — the strong jus soli tradition, the early recognition of universal personhood within the nation’s borders, and the complex relationship with France (which imposed a debt of 150 million francs for “lost property” — including enslaved people — which Haiti paid until 1947).

Diaspora Law and the 2012 Amendment

The Haitian diaspora — estimated at 3–4 million people worldwide (comparable to Haiti’s urban population) — is concentrated in:

  • United States: Florida (Miami), New York, Massachusetts, New Jersey
  • Canada: Montreal (largest Francophone Haitian community outside Haiti)
  • France: Paris metropolitan area
  • Dominican Republic: Large labor migration community (legally complex situation)
  • Other Caribbean islands: Bahamas, Cuba, Martinique, Guadeloupe

Annual remittances of $3+ billion USD represent approximately 25–30% of Haiti’s GDP — one of the highest remittance-to-GDP ratios in the world. The 2012 dual citizenship amendment was explicitly tied to the government’s recognition of the diaspora’s economic importance.

Constitutional and Political Instability

Haiti has experienced severe political instability. Key events affecting nationality administration:

  • 2010 earthquake: Killed approximately 230,000 people and devastated government infrastructure, including records and civil registry systems.
  • 2021 presidential assassination: President Jovenel Moïse was assassinated in July 2021, deepening the political crisis.
  • Gang control of territory: By 2023–2024, criminal gangs controlled significant portions of Port-au-Prince and other areas, severely limiting government function.
  • UN-mandated Multinational Security Support Mission (authorized 2023, deployed 2024) — led by Kenya.

These conditions have severely impacted the ability of the Office National d’Identification (ONI) and the MICT to process nationality applications in a timely manner. Applicants should expect significant delays and are advised to work through Haitian consulates in their country of residence where possible.

African Descent Heritage Pathways

The Haitian Constitution has historically had provisions recognizing a special relationship with the African diaspora. The 1987 Constitution originally contained language suggesting that persons “of African descent” could acquire Haitian nationality under certain conditions. This pathway is theoretically available but has not been systematically implemented.

Investment Pathways

Citizenship by Investment (CBI)

Haiti does not currently operate a formal Citizenship by Investment program. The political and institutional context makes a structured CBI program impractical at present.

Residency by Investment (RBI)

No formal RBI program exists. The Centre de Facilitation des Investissements (CFI) is the government agency responsible for facilitating investment, but it does not administer a structured investor residency program.

Investors who wish to establish residency in Haiti must use the standard work permit and residence permit system through the MICT. Given Haiti’s current instability, most investment activity by foreigners is channeled through NGOs, development organizations, or diaspora networks rather than direct individual investment migration.

Business & Entrepreneurship

Company Formation

Haiti’s legal system derives from Napoleonic (French civil) law. Business structures available:

  • Société Anonyme (SA): Equivalent to a corporation/public company. Standard for larger enterprises.
  • Société en Nom Collectif (SNC): General partnership.
  • Société en Commandite: Limited partnership.
  • Entreprise Individuelle: Sole proprietorship.

Companies are registered at the Registre du Commerce et des Sociétés and with the Ministère du Commerce et de l’Industrie.

Entrepreneur & Startup Visas

No dedicated entrepreneur visa exists. Foreign business owners must obtain a permis de séjour (residence permit) and a permis de travail (work permit) through the MICT.

Haiti’s tax administration capacity is severely limited by institutional challenges. The Direction Générale des Impôts (DGI) is the revenue authority. Tax collection rates are among the lowest in the Western Hemisphere as a percentage of GDP.

Banking & Financial Infrastructure

The Banque de la République d’Haïti (BRH) is the central bank. Commercial banking includes:

  • SOGEBANK (Société Générale Haïtienne de Banque): Largest bank.
  • Unibank: Major commercial bank with strong diaspora connections.
  • BNC (Banque Nationale de Crédit): State-owned bank.
  • BPH (Banque Populaire Haïtienne): State development bank.
  • Capital Bank: Smaller commercial institution.
  • MonCash / Digicel: Mobile money platform — extremely important given limited banking access outside Port-au-Prince.

Haiti uses the Haitian Gourde (HTG) as its currency. USD is widely used in commerce and is the de facto standard for larger transactions. Remittance infrastructure (Western Union, MoneyGram, direct transfers) is highly developed.

Key Programs & Incentives

  • Investment Code: Provides tax holidays of 5–15 years for qualifying investments in priority sectors.
  • Free Zones: Several free trade zones operate in Haiti, including the Parc Industriel CARACOL (CODEVI) in northern Haiti, which has attracted garment manufacturing investment from Korean and other companies.
  • CFI (Centre de Facilitation des Investissements): Facilitates investment applications and provides information.
  • USAID/development finance: Significant international development finance activity in Haiti from USAID, IFC, Inter-American Development Bank.
  • Garment and textile: Haiti benefits from HOPE/HELP Acts (US legislation) providing duty-free access to the US market for Haitian garment exports.

Loss of Nationality

Voluntary Renunciation

A Haitian citizen of full age may renounce their nationality by making a formal declaration at the MICT or at a Haitian consulate. The renunciation is registered and takes effect upon formal acceptance. As with most jurisdictions, a stateless outcome is to be avoided.

Involuntary Loss

Since the 2012 amendment, the grounds for involuntary loss are narrower:

  • Fraud or misrepresentation: Nationality obtained through fraud may be annulled by the authorities.
  • Service to a foreign power against Haitian interests: A person who voluntarily serves in the armed forces of a state at war with Haiti, or who provides material assistance to a foreign power acting against Haiti, may face loss of nationality.
  • Acquisition of foreign nationality no longer triggers automatic loss (post-2012 amendment). This is the most significant change from the 1987 regime.

Recovery / Resumption

For persons who lost Haitian nationality under the 1987 prohibition (Article 15 of the original 1987 Constitution) by naturalizing abroad:

  • The 2012 constitutional amendment created a pathway for such persons to reclaim their Haitian nationality.
  • Applications are submitted to the MICT or through Haitian consulates.
  • Documentation of Haitian birth or parentage, plus identity documents, are required.
  • Processing times are variable given institutional capacity constraints.

This resumption pathway is one of the most frequently used features of Haitian nationality law, given the size and geographic spread of the diaspora.

Practical Guide

Documents Needed

For naturalization:

  • Valid passport (all relevant passports)
  • Birth certificate
  • Police clearance from Haiti (Direction de la Police Judiciaire or commissariat) and country of origin
  • Proof of lawful residence in Haiti (residence permits, entry/exit records)
  • Proof of address in Haiti
  • Evidence of means of support
  • Marriage certificate (if applicable)
  • Two character references from Haitian citizens
  • Passport photographs
  • Completed application form (MICT)

For descent registration:

  • Applicant’s birth certificate
  • Haitian parent’s birth certificate and nationality documentation
  • If parent’s nationality was affected by 1987 rule: additional documentation of current status

For resumption post-2012 amendment:

  • Prior Haitian nationality documentation (birth certificate, old passport, ONI card)
  • Documentation showing when and how Haitian nationality was lost
  • Current foreign passport
  • Declaration of intent to resume Haitian nationality

Where to Submit

Primary office: Ministère de l’Intérieur et des Collectivités Territoriales (MICT) Port-au-Prince, Haiti

Also: Office National d’Identification (ONI) for identity card issuance

Given ongoing security conditions in Port-au-Prince, applicants are strongly advised to:

  1. Contact the MICT by phone or email before attempting to visit in person.
  2. Use Haitian consulates in their country of residence where possible.
  3. Work through authorized legal representatives (notaires or attorneys).

Haitian Embassies/Consulates with significant nationality processing capacity:

  • Embassy of Haiti in Washington D.C.
  • Consulate General of Haiti in Miami, New York, Boston, Chicago
  • Embassy of Haiti in Ottawa
  • Embassy of Haiti in Paris
  • Consulate General of Haiti in Montreal

Processing Time

Given Haiti’s institutional challenges:

  • Standard naturalization: 18–36 months (highly variable)
  • Diaspora nationality resumption: 12–24 months
  • Consular registration of birth/descent: 6–18 months

Applicants should maintain copies of all submitted documents and keep written records of all communications with officials.

Fees

  • Naturalization application: approximately USD 100–300
  • Administrative processing fees at consulates: vary by location (USD 50–200)
  • ONI identity card issuance: small fee
  • Notarial fees (if documents require notarization in Haiti): variable

Related Countries

  • Dominican Republic — shares the island of Hispaniola; deeply intertwined and complex bilateral relationship; Haitian labor migration; persistent tensions over borders and nationality
  • Cuba — Caribbean neighbor; historical solidarity during Cuban Revolution; significant Cuban medical presence in Haiti
  • France — former colonial power; French is an official language; large Haitian diaspora in France; colonial debt history
  • United States — largest destination for Haitian diaspora; major political and humanitarian relationship; HOPE/HELP Acts trade preference
  • Canada — large Haitian community especially in Quebec (French-speaking); significant development aid
  • Jamaica — CARICOM partner; Caribbean solidarity history
