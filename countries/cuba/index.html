Dual Citizenship

Cuba’s position on dual citizenship is one of the most legally complex in the Western Hemisphere.

The formal legal position: Cuba does not recognize dual nationality. The Cuban government treats Cuban-born persons (and their descendants) as Cuban nationals regardless of any other citizenship they may have acquired. This principle is enshrined in Article 34 of the Constitution of the Republic of Cuba (2019) and in the Nationality Law (Law No. 1312 of 1976, as amended).

The practical reality: A large Cuban diaspora — numbering over 2 million, with the largest concentration in the United States — holds both Cuban and foreign (principally US) citizenship simultaneously. The Cuban government requires Cuban-born persons to enter and exit Cuba on a Cuban passport, effectively ignoring their foreign nationality upon entry into Cuban territory. From Cuba’s perspective, once on Cuban soil, a Cuban-born person is solely a Cuban national subject to Cuban law, regardless of any US or other passport they carry.

Key implications:

  • Cuban-Americans who travel to Cuba must use their Cuban passport to enter — the US passport is not recognized by Cuban immigration for Cuban-born travelers
  • Cuba does not recognize the US government’s consular protection of Cuban-born US citizens while they are on Cuban territory
  • Acquisition of a foreign nationality by a Cuban citizen does not legally terminate Cuban nationality under Cuban law — Cuba never releases its claim
  • Conversely, a Cuban national who wishes to be released from Cuban citizenship must formally renounce it through consular channels — a process that has historically been difficult and rarely used

2019 Constitutional update: The 2019 Constitution maintained Cuba’s non-recognition of dual nationality but made some adjustments to rights of Cubans abroad.

Acquisition of Nationality

By Birth (Jus Soli)

Cuba applies jus soli to all persons born on Cuban territory, with no major exceptions for children of diplomats or transiting foreigners (unlike many other civil law countries).

Any person born in Cuba is a Cuban national by birth. This applies regardless of the nationality, legal status, or immigration status of the parents at the time of birth.

Reference: Constitución de la República de Cuba (2019), Art. 33; Ley de Nacionalidad No. 1312 (1976).

By Descent (Jus Sanguinis)

Children born abroad to at least one Cuban parent (by birth or naturalization) are entitled to Cuban nationality.

ScenarioEligibility
One Cuban parent (father or mother)Yes — entitled to Cuban nationality
Both parents Cuban nationalsYes — automatic entitlement
Born abroad to naturalized Cuban parentYes — eligible
Second generation (grandparent Cuban)Not automatic; requires petition and special circumstances
Born outside Cuba but registered at Cuban consulateYes — registration confers nationality

Registration of children born abroad to Cuban parents must be done at the relevant Cuban consulate or diplomatic mission. There is no age limit imposed by law for claiming descent, though adult claims require more extensive documentation.

Reference: Ley de Nacionalidad No. 1312 (1976), Art. 10.

By Adoption

Foreign minors legally adopted by Cuban nationals may acquire Cuban nationality. Requirements include:

  • Final adoption decree issued by Cuban courts
  • Application to the Civil Registry (Registro Civil) coordinated through the Ministry of Justice
  • Proof that adoptive parents are Cuban nationals

The Cuban state has historically controlled the adoption process tightly. International adoption from Cuba has been effectively unavailable since the 1990s due to policy restrictions.

By Marriage

Cuba’s nationality law does not provide an accelerated or automatic pathway to nationality through marriage to a Cuban citizen. A foreign national married to a Cuban citizen must go through the standard naturalization process.

However, marriage to a Cuban national may assist in obtaining Cuban residency (permission to reside in Cuba), which is a precondition for naturalization. The Cuban government strictly controls residency permissions for foreigners.

By Naturalization (Residency)

Standard Requirements

Naturalization in Cuba is extraordinarily rare in practice. The Cuban state exercises near-absolute discretion over naturalization decisions and grants citizenship only in exceptional circumstances.

RequirementDetail

Critical note on rarity: Naturalization in Cuba is not a routine administrative process. The Cuban government does not actively encourage foreign nationals to become Cuban citizens, and most applications from non-Cuban-origin foreigners result in indefinite processing delays or denial. Cuba’s controlled economy and political system mean there is little structural demand for foreign naturalization. Exceptions have been made for:

  • Prominent foreign sympathizers of the Cuban Revolution (historically)
  • Spouses of Cuban nationals after extended periods of residence
  • Intelligence operatives and other special cases

Reduced Residency Categories

Unlike Panama, Ecuador, or the Dominican Republic, Cuba does not offer reduced naturalization periods for Ibero-American nationals, Spanish nationals, or any other special category of foreign nationals in the standard legal framework. The 5-year period applies across the board, subject to the exceptional and discretionary nature of Cuban naturalization grants.

Preferred Pathways

Given Cuba’s political and economic constraints, traditional “preferred pathways” to citizenship are largely unavailable. However, the following are relevant:

  1. Birth on Cuban territory (jus soli): The most certain path — any person born in Cuba is a Cuban national.

  2. Descent from Cuban parent: The most practical path for members of the Cuban diaspora and their children. Registration at a Cuban consulate is possible for those with at least one Cuban parent.

  3. The Cuban Adjustment Act (US-specific): This is not a Cuban law — it is a US federal statute (Public Law 89-732, 1966) that allows Cuban nationals who enter the United States and are physically present for at least one year to apply for lawful permanent residence (a “green card”). The Cuban Adjustment Act remains in effect as of 2026 but has been subject to periodic political debate. It does not affect Cuban nationality but is the primary mechanism by which Cuban nationals obtain US residency.

  4. Wet foot / dry foot policy (historical): The informal US policy of permitting Cubans who reached US soil to remain while returning those intercepted at sea was officially ended by the Obama administration in January 2017. Cubans intercepted at sea are now subject to return.

  5. Residence through marriage: For foreigners married to Cubans who physically reside in Cuba, long-term residence authorization is the first step — naturalization remains at state discretion after 5 years.

Special Clauses & Programs

The “Cuban Citizen” Presumption

Cuba’s most distinctive rule: any person born in Cuba, or born abroad to a Cuban parent, is presumed by Cuban law to be a Cuban national regardless of any other citizenship held. This means:

  • Entering Cuba on any other passport does not override Cuban nationality claims
  • Cuban authorities may treat dual nationals as solely Cuban while in Cuba
  • This has implications for consular protection — the US State Department warns US citizens of Cuban origin that they may be subject to Cuban jurisdiction without access to US consular services

Emigration Controls (Historically)

Cuba historically required its citizens to obtain an emigration permit (tarjeta blanca or “white card”) and an exit permit before leaving the country. This system was a major restriction on freedom of movement.

In January 2013, Cuba reformed its emigration laws under Decreto-Ley No. 302 (2012):

  • The requirement for an exit permit was abolished
  • Cuban citizens can now travel abroad with only a valid Cuban passport
  • Cuban nationals can remain abroad for up to 24 months (extendable) before being considered to have “abandoned” Cuban residence
  • Cubans living abroad for more than 24 months without extension may lose certain rights associated with residency (housing, etc.) but do not automatically lose nationality

Cuban Passport Requirement for Reentry

Cuban nationals born in Cuba must enter and exit Cuba using a Cuban passport — regardless of any other nationality they hold. Attempting to enter Cuba on a foreign passport when Cuban-born will result in the Cuban passport being required by authorities.

Political Asylum and Loss of Protection

Cubans who are granted political asylum in a third country do not lose Cuban nationality — but Cuba may consider their asylum status as a hostile act and restrict consular services accordingly.

Investment Pathways

Citizenship by Investment (CBI)

Cuba has no citizenship-by-investment program. No investment, regardless of amount, creates a pathway to Cuban citizenship outside the standard naturalization process.

Residency by Investment (RBI)

Cuba does not have a formal residency-by-investment program available to most foreigners. However:

  • Joint Ventures (Empresas Mixtas): Foreign investors approved under Cuba’s Foreign Investment Law (Law No. 118 of 2014) may obtain extended residency authorization as a component of their business arrangement
  • Special Development Zones: The Mariel Special Development Zone (ZEDM) has attracted some foreign investment, and approved investors may receive extended residence authorizations
  • Investment does not automatically create a path to permanent residency or nationality

Business & Entrepreneurship

Company Formation

Cuba’s economy remains largely state-controlled. The landscape for private enterprise has evolved since the 2021 legalization of private small and medium-sized enterprises (MiPymes), but remains highly constrained.

Critical limitation: All foreign investment requires approval from Cuban state authorities, and the process is opaque and lengthy. The Cuban state retains priority in most sectors.

Entrepreneur & Startup Visas

Cuba does not have a startup or entrepreneur visa in the conventional sense. Foreign entrepreneurs who wish to operate in Cuba must do so through the joint venture or foreign capital company frameworks, which require governmental authorization.

Banking & Financial Infrastructure

  • Cuba operates a state-controlled banking system
  • Banco Nacional de Cuba (BNC) — central bank
  • Banco Internacional de Comercio S.A. (BICSA) — handles international transactions
  • US financial sanctions (OFAC regulations) severely restrict banking transactions involving Cuba from/to the United States
  • Cuban banks are largely cut off from the SWIFT international payment system
  • The MLC (Moneda Libremente Convertible) system operates in hard currency shops but has created a two-tier economy
  • Foreign bank accounts in Cuba are difficult to open and maintain

Key Programs & Incentives

  • Mariel Special Development Zone (ZEDM): 8% corporate tax rate (versus standard 35%); 10-year tax holiday for new investments; located 45 km west of Havana with deep-water port
  • Tourism sector: Priority sector for foreign investment; joint ventures with Cuban tourism ministry entities
  • Biotechnology & Pharmaceuticals: Cuba has a developed biotech sector (CIGB, Finlay Institute) with potential for collaborative ventures
  • Law 118 of 2014: Governs foreign investment; guarantees against expropriation without compensation; repatriation of profits allowed

Loss of Nationality

Voluntary Renunciation

A Cuban national may formally renounce Cuban citizenship through:

  • Formal declaration before a Cuban consular or diplomatic mission
  • Application to the Ministry of Justice
  • The process has historically been bureaucratically difficult and slow

Key distinction: Cuba does not strip Cuban nationality from nationals who acquire foreign citizenship — it simply ignores the foreign citizenship. The person remains Cuban under Cuban law.

Recovery / Resumption

Former Cuban nationals who renounced or lost Cuban citizenship may petition for resumption of nationality through MINREX. The process is lengthy and subject to political considerations.
