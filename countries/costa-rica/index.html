Dual Citizenship

Yes — Costa Rica fully recognizes dual and multiple citizenship. The 1995 reform to Article 16 of the Constitution removed the prior requirement that naturalized citizens renounce their original nationality.

Prior to 1995, Costa Rica required renunciation of prior citizenship as a condition of naturalization. The constitutional amendment eliminated this requirement entirely, meaning both Costa Rican nationals by birth and by naturalization may hold additional citizenships without restriction.

Key points:

  • Costa Ricans who acquire foreign citizenship do not lose their Costa Rican nationality
  • Foreign nationals who naturalize in Costa Rica are not required to renounce their prior citizenship
  • Costa Rica does not notify foreign governments when their nationals naturalize as Costa Rican
  • Children born in Costa Rica to foreign parents hold Costa Rican nationality alongside any nationality inherited from their parents

Acquisition of Nationality

By Birth (Jus Soli)

Costa Rica applies unrestricted jus soli. Any person born on Costa Rican territory acquires Costa Rican nationality by birth, regardless of the nationality or immigration status of their parents.

Constitutional basis: Article 13(1) of the Political Constitution of the Republic of Costa Rica.

This includes:

  • Children born anywhere within Costa Rican national territory (including territorial waters and airspace)
  • Children born to parents in irregular immigration status — Costa Rica does not restrict birthright citizenship based on the parents’ legal status
  • Children born on Costa Rican-flagged vessels or aircraft

Costa Rica is one of the few countries worldwide that maintains fully unrestricted jus soli with no parental residency or legal status requirements.

By Descent (Jus Sanguinis)

Costa Rican nationality by birth also extends to children born abroad to Costa Rican parents:

ScenarioRequirementConstitutional Basis
Born abroad to a Costa Rican father or mother (by birth)At least one parent is a Costa Rican national by birthArt. 13(2)
Born abroad to a Costa Rican father or mother (by naturalization)At least one parent is a naturalized Costa Rican, provided the child registers before age 25Art. 13(2)

Registration requirement: Children born abroad to Costa Rican parents must be registered at a Costa Rican consulate or at the Registro Civil (Civil Registry) in San Jose. For children of naturalized Costa Ricans, registration must occur before the child reaches 25 years of age. For children of Costa Ricans by birth, there is no strict deadline, though timely registration is recommended.

Generational limit: The Constitution does not impose an explicit generational cutoff for children of Costa Ricans by birth. However, the child of a naturalized citizen must register before turning 25, or they may need to pursue naturalization instead.

By Adoption

Adoption does not automatically confer Costa Rican nationality. However:

  • A foreign child adopted by Costa Rican nationals may acquire Costa Rican nationality if the adoption is formally recognized by Costa Rican courts and the child is registered with the Registro Civil
  • Minor children adopted through full adoption (adopcion plena) under Costa Rican family law are treated as biological children for nationality purposes
  • The child must be registered before the age of 25 if the adopting parent is a naturalized citizen

By Marriage

Marriage to a Costa Rican national does not automatically confer nationality. However, it creates a pathway to naturalization with specific conditions:

By Naturalization (Residency)

Foreign nationals who have resided legally in Costa Rica may apply for naturalization through the Registro Civil, which is administered by the Tribunal Supremo de Elecciones (TSE).

Special Clauses & Programs

Honorary Citizenship

Under Article 14(6) of the Constitution, the Costa Rican Legislative Assembly (Asamblea Legislativa) may grant honorary citizenship to foreigners who have rendered outstanding service to the Republic. This is a rare distinction conferred at the discretion of the legislature, with no residency requirement.

Nationality for Children Born Abroad

Costa Rica is particularly generous in extending nationality to children born abroad. Article 13(2) ensures that children of Costa Ricans by birth have an unconditional right to Costa Rican nationality regardless of where they are born. This right does not expire and can be exercised at any age by registering at a consulate or the Registro Civil.

Central American Integration

As a member of the Central American Integration System (SICA), Costa Rica participates in regional mobility frameworks. Nationals of CA-4 countries (Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua) and Panama enjoy facilitated entry and movement, though Costa Rica maintains its own border controls separate from the CA-4 free mobility zone.

No Citizenship by Investment Program

Costa Rica does not offer a formal citizenship-by-investment (CBI) or “golden passport” program. See Investment Pathways below for residency options that can lead to eventual naturalization.

Investment Pathways

Citizenship by Investment (CBI)

Not available. Costa Rica does not offer a direct path from investment to citizenship. All naturalization requires physical residency for the required number of years and meeting the language, cultural knowledge, and character requirements.

Golden Visa / Residency by Investment (RBI)

Available. Costa Rica offers several residency categories that function as investment-based pathways to eventual naturalization:

PathwayRequirementsVisa TypeNotes
Inversionista (Investor)Investment of at least USD 150,000 in real estate, business, or approved projectTemporary Residency (2 years, renewable)Most common investment-based pathway; can invest in property, stocks, or business ventures
Rentista (Income Earner)Guaranteed monthly income of at least USD 2,500 for a minimum of 2 years (deposit of USD 60,000 or proof of stable income)Temporary Residency (2 years, renewable)Must demonstrate income from a pension, investments, or foreign employment; funds must be deposited monthly in a Costa Rican bank
Pensionado (Retiree)Monthly pension or retirement income of at least USD 1,000 from a permanent sourceTemporary Residency (2 years, renewable)Must prove the pension comes from a recognized retirement system; one of the most popular expat pathways
Business ownerEstablish and operate a business in Costa RicaTemporary ResidencyMust demonstrate the business is operational and generating economic activity

Important: The Inversionista threshold was raised from USD 200,000 to the current level as part of immigration reforms. The Rentista and Pensionado programs are among the most popular in Latin America for retirees and remote workers. Always verify current thresholds at Direccion General de Migracion y Extranjeria (DGME).

Path to citizenship: Temporary residency (2 years, renewable) can transition to permanent residency after 3 years of temporary residency. Once a foreign national accumulates the required total years of legal residency (7 standard, 5 for preferred nationalities, or 3 if married to a Costa Rican with children), they may apply for naturalization.

Business & Entrepreneurship

Costa Rica stands out in Central America as a stable democracy with no military (abolished in 1948), strong environmental policies, and a well-educated workforce. It has attracted major foreign investment from companies like Intel, Amazon, HP, and Procter & Gamble, particularly in technology, medical devices, and shared services. The country’s free trade zones, strategic Pacific/Atlantic access, and CAFTA-DR membership make it a competitive base for business in the Americas.

Company Formation

Most common entity typeS.A. (Sociedad Anonima — Corporation)
Formation timeline2-4 weeks
Formation cost300,000-600,000 CRC (~USD 550-1,100) including notary and registration fees
Minimum capitalNo statutory minimum (nominal amounts accepted)
Online registrationPartial — notarization and Registro Nacional filing required

The Sociedad Anonima (S.A.) is by far the most common business entity in Costa Rica, used for everything from small businesses to large corporations. It requires a minimum of two shareholders (though nominees can be used) and a board of three directors. The S.R.L. (Sociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada) is an alternative with simpler governance but is less commonly used. Formation requires a Costa Rican notary public (notario publico), registration with the Registro Nacional, and obtaining a cedula juridica (corporate tax ID). Foreign nationals may own 100% of a Costa Rican company without restriction in most sectors.

Annual corporate tax: All S.A.s and S.R.L.s must pay an annual corporate registration fee (impuesto a personas juridicas) based on their income level, ranging from approximately 15% to 50% of the base salary (salario base), typically CRC 70,000-230,000 per year (~USD 130-425).

Entrepreneur & Startup Visas

Costa Rica does not have a dedicated startup or entrepreneur visa. Common pathways:

  • Inversionista (Investor) Residency: Invest USD 150,000 in a Costa Rican business or project; grants temporary residency with work authorization limited to managing the investment
  • Rentista Residency: Demonstrate USD 2,500/month in foreign income; allows residency but not employment by a Costa Rican company (self-employment and remote work are permitted in practice)
  • Pensionado Residency: USD 1,000/month pension income; same work restrictions as Rentista
  • Work Permit (Permiso de Trabajo): Tied to a specific employer; requires the company to demonstrate that no qualified Costa Rican is available for the position
  • Special Category — Representante de Casa Extranjera: For representatives of foreign companies establishing operations in Costa Rica

Note on work authorization: Rentista and Pensionado residents technically cannot work for a Costa Rican employer, but they can own and manage their own businesses, receive investment income, and work remotely for foreign companies. The Inversionista category explicitly allows managing the investment.

Tax Overview

Corporate tax rate30% (standard); 20% for income between CRC 5.7M-8.6M; 10% for income between CRC 0-5.7M; 5-15% for companies in Free Trade Zones
VAT (IVA)13% (standard); 4% on private health services; 2% on pharmaceuticals and basic supplies; 1% on staple foods
Capital gains tax15% on capital gains from asset sales
Personal income tax (top rate)25% (above CRC 5.2M/month, ~USD 9,600/month)
Withholding tax on dividends15%
Tax treaty networkLimited — treaties with Spain, Germany, and a few others; expanding
Territorial taxationCosta Rica uses a territorial tax system — only income sourced within Costa Rica is taxed; foreign-source income is generally not taxed

The territorial tax system is a major draw for international entrepreneurs and remote workers. Income earned from foreign sources (foreign clients, foreign investments, foreign employment) is generally not subject to Costa Rican income tax. This makes Costa Rica especially attractive for digital nomads, remote workers, and businesses that primarily serve international markets.

Free Trade Zones (Zonas Francas): Companies operating within designated free trade zones enjoy significant tax incentives including 100% exemption from corporate income tax for the first 8 years (50% for the following 4 years), exemption from import duties and taxes on raw materials and equipment, and exemption from export taxes.

Banking & Financial Infrastructure

  • Major banks: Banco Nacional de Costa Rica (state-owned, largest), Banco de Costa Rica (BCR, state-owned), BAC Creamer (private, largest private bank), Scotiabank Costa Rica, Davivienda
  • State-owned banks (Banco Nacional, BCR, Banco Popular) offer deposit guarantees backed by the government
  • SINPE — Costa Rica’s national electronic payment system (interbank transfers)
  • SINPE Movil — Mobile payment system linked to phone numbers (widely adopted, similar to Venmo/Zelle)
  • Costa Rican colon (CRC) is the national currency; USD is widely accepted in tourist areas and for real estate transactions
  • Opening a bank account as a foreigner requires residency (cedula de residencia) or a cedula juridica (for companies); some banks accept passport-only accounts with limitations
  • Currency controls: Costa Rica has a managed float exchange rate system; no significant capital controls for foreign investors

Key Programs & Incentives

  • PROCOMER (Promotora del Comercio Exterior) — Export promotion agency providing market intelligence, trade missions, and export development support
  • CINDE (Coalicion Costarricense de Iniciativas de Desarrollo) — Investment promotion agency that facilitates foreign direct investment, particularly in technology, medical devices, and shared services
  • Zona Franca (Free Trade Zone) regime — Tax incentives for companies in designated zones (especially in manufacturing, services, and technology)
  • CAFTA-DR — Free trade agreement with the United States, Central America, and the Dominican Republic; preferential access to the US market
  • EU Association Agreement — Preferential trade access to European markets
  • Startup Costa Rica — Growing ecosystem with government support programs for innovation and technology startups
  • Auge UCR — University of Costa Rica’s business incubator and accelerator
  • Parque Tec — Technology park and incubator in Cartago, affiliated with Instituto Tecnologico de Costa Rica (TEC)
  • San Jose metro area — Primary business hub; Gran Area Metropolitana (GAM) houses ~60% of the population and the vast majority of business activity
  • Guanacaste — Growing hub for tourism-related businesses and remote work communities
  • Medical devices cluster — Costa Rica is the largest medical device exporter in Latin America, with companies like Abbott, Baxter, and Boston Scientific operating manufacturing facilities

Loss of Nationality

Voluntary Renunciation

  • Nationals by birth: May renounce Costa Rican nationality by formal declaration. However, Costa Rica generally discourages renunciation and the process requires formal petition to the Registro Civil.
  • Naturalized citizens: May voluntarily renounce by formal declaration before the Registro Civil.

Involuntary Loss

Nationals by birth: Under the Constitution, Costa Rican nationality acquired by birth is extremely difficult to lose involuntarily. Acquiring another nationality does not cause loss of Costa Rican nationality (per the 1995 reform). However, Costa Rican nationality by birth may be affected if:

CauseDetail
Voluntary acquisition of another nationality (pre-1995)Before the 1995 reform, acquiring foreign citizenship could cause loss; this no longer applies
Fraudulent registrationIf the birth registration was obtained through fraud

Naturalized citizens may lose their Costa Rican nationality in the following cases (Article 15 of the Constitution):

CauseDetail
Voluntary renunciationFormal declaration of renunciation
Absence from Costa Rica for prolonged periodResiding abroad for more than 7 consecutive years without authorization (unless in service of Costa Rica, international organizations, or for health/study reasons)
Resuming use of prior nationality in ways incompatible with Costa Rican citizenshipUsing prior nationality to claim diplomatic protection against Costa Rica
FraudIf the naturalization was obtained through false information, concealment, or fraud
Acts against the sovereignty or independence of Costa RicaEngaging in activities that undermine the State

Recovery / Resumption

ScenarioProcess
Costa Rican by birth who renounced or lost nationalityMay recover nationality by establishing domicile in Costa Rica and declaring intent to recover before the Registro Civil
Costa Rican by birth (pre-1995 loss due to foreign naturalization)May recover by declaration at the Registro Civil or a consulate; the 1995 reform retroactively protects birth nationals
Naturalized Costa Rican who lost nationalityMust apply for naturalization again from scratch; no expedited recovery

Practical Guide

Documents Needed

For naturalization by residency (standard checklist):

  • Completed application form (Solicitud de Naturalizacion)
  • Valid passport (original + certified copy)
  • Costa Rican residency card (cedula de residencia) — original + copy
  • Birth certificate (original, apostilled or legalized, translated into Spanish by an official translator)
  • Marriage certificate (if applying under marriage category — apostilled, translated)
  • Spouse’s Costa Rican nationality documentation (if applying under marriage category)
  • Birth certificates of children (if applying for reduced residency based on having children with Costa Rican spouse)
  • Criminal background check from country of origin (apostilled, translated, recent — typically within 6 months)
  • Criminal background check from Costa Rica (issued by the Poder Judicial)
  • Certificate of good conduct from local police
  • Health certificate from a Costa Rican licensed physician
  • Proof of means of subsistence (employment letter, business registration, bank statements, tax returns)
  • Proof of continuous residency (utility bills, lease agreements, bank statements, CCSS enrollment)
  • Two recent passport-sized photographs (white background)
  • Receipt of application fee payment (timbres fiscales)
  • Proof of Spanish language proficiency (evaluated during the process)
  • Proof of knowledge of Costa Rican history and values (evaluated during the process)

For registration of nationality by descent (born abroad to Costa Rican parent):

  • Birth certificate of the applicant (apostilled, translated)
  • Birth certificate of the Costa Rican parent
  • Costa Rican parent’s cedula de identidad or passport
  • Valid passport or ID of the applicant
  • Two passport-sized photographs

Exams/Assessment: The Spanish language and cultural knowledge assessments are administered by the TSE as part of the naturalization process. The cultural assessment covers Costa Rican history, democratic values, national symbols, geography, and civic principles. Study materials may be available from the TSE.
