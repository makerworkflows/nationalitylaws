Dual Citizenship

Yes — France fully permits dual (and multiple) citizenship. There is no requirement to renounce a foreign nationality when acquiring French citizenship, and acquiring a foreign nationality does not automatically result in loss of French nationality. This has been the position since the 1973 reform of the Civil Code nationality provisions. France does not limit the number of citizenships a person may hold simultaneously.

Acquisition of Nationality

French nationality can be acquired by birth, descent, adoption, marriage, naturalization (residency), or special declaration. The Civil Code distinguishes between nationality of origin (attribution) and acquired nationality.

By Birth (Jus Soli)

France operates a system of conditional jus soli — birth on French territory alone does not automatically confer nationality at birth (with one exception). The rules are:

Double Jus Soli (Automatic at Birth) A child born in France is French at birth if at least one parent was also born in France, regardless of the parents’ nationality. This is the “double jus soli” rule (Article 19-3). This applies even if the parent born in France is not French. This is the only scenario where birth on French soil alone creates French nationality at birth.

Simple Jus Soli (Automatic at Age 18) A child born in France to two foreign parents acquires French nationality automatically at age 18 if:

  • They are still residing in France at age 18, AND
  • They have had their habitual residence in France for a continuous or cumulative period of at least 5 years since age 11

This is governed by Article 21-7. The acquisition is automatic — no declaration or application is needed. However, the person may decline French nationality by declaration within 6 months before turning 18 or within 12 months after (Article 21-8).

Early Claim by Declaration (Ages 13-18)

  • Age 16-18: The child may claim French nationality by personal declaration if they have resided in France for at least 5 years since age 11 (Article 21-11).
  • Age 13-16: The parents may claim French nationality on the child’s behalf if the child has had habitual residence in France for at least 5 years since age 8 (Article 21-11). The child’s consent is required.

Stateless Children (Safety Net) A child born in France who would otherwise be stateless is French at birth (Article 19-1), in accordance with France’s obligations under the 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness.

Overseas Territories The jus soli rules apply equally in all French overseas departments and territories (Guadeloupe, Martinique, Reunion, French Guiana, Mayotte, New Caledonia, French Polynesia, etc.). Birth in any French territory counts as birth in France.

Mayotte Exception: Since 2018, stricter rules apply in Mayotte. For a child born in Mayotte to foreign parents, at least one parent must have been lawfully and continuously resident in France for at least 3 months before the birth for jus soli to apply (Article 2493-1 of the Civil Code, introduced by the 2018 law on controlled immigration).

By Descent (Jus Sanguinis)

Automatic at Birth A child is French at birth if at least one parent is French at the time of the child’s birth (Article 18). This applies regardless of where the child is born. There is no generational limit written into the statute, but practical proof requirements apply.

Key Rules:

  • Both legitimate and natural (out-of-wedlock) children qualify, provided filiation is legally established.
  • If filiation to the French parent is established after birth, the child is deemed to have been French from birth once filiation is established, provided it is established during minority.
  • If filiation is established after the child reaches majority, the child may claim French nationality by declaration within a specified period.
  • Adopted children: see “By Adoption” below.

Proof and Registration:

  • For children born abroad, nationality is documented via a Certificate of French Nationality (CNF) issued by the Tribunal Judiciaire of Paris (Service de la Nationalite des Francais nes et etablis hors de France).
  • The French parent must be able to prove an unbroken chain of French nationality. In practice, this means producing CNFs, birth certificates, or nationality documents going back to the last person in the chain who was born in France or acquired nationality by another means.

Generational Limit in Practice (Article 30-3): A person born abroad to a French parent who was also born abroad may lose the presumption of French nationality if neither they nor the French parent have ever had any link to France (such as registration at a consulate, possession of French identity documents, or habitual residence in France). If no such link exists for 50 years, the person may be considered to have lost French nationality. This rarely applies in practice but is a theoretical limit.

By Adoption

Full Adoption (Adoption Pleniere) A child who is the subject of a full adoption by a French national is French from birth (Article 20). Full adoption severs the legal ties with the biological family and creates a new filiation equivalent to biological filiation. The adopted child is treated exactly as a biological child for nationality purposes.

Simple Adoption (Adoption Simple) A child who is the subject of a simple adoption by a French national does not automatically acquire French nationality. However, the child may:

  • Acquire French nationality by declaration during minority (Article 21-12), provided they have resided in France for at least 5 years, OR
  • Apply for naturalization as an adult under the normal rules (with the residency requirement potentially reduced)

Simple adoption does not sever ties with the biological family and does not create the same nationality consequences as full adoption.

By Marriage

A foreign national married to a French citizen may acquire French nationality by declaration (not naturalization — this is an important distinction). The conditions are:

Requirements (Article 21-1 through 21-6):

RequirementDetails
Duration of Marriage4 years of marriage at the time of declaration
Extended to 5 Years IfThe foreign spouse cannot prove 3 years of continuous residence in France since the marriage, OR the French spouse was not registered at a French consulate during the marriage while the couple lived abroad
Community of LifeContinuous affective and material community of life (communaute de vie) since the marriage. Must not have ceased at the time of declaration.
LanguageB1 level (oral) in French, assessed by TEF, TCF, DELF B1, or equivalent diploma
Good CharacterNo conviction for terrorism, crimes against vital interests of the nation, or sentences of 6+ months unsuspended imprisonment
KnowledgeSufficient knowledge of rights and duties conferred by French nationality, and of the essential principles and values of the Republic

Process:

  1. Declaration is filed at the local prefecture (if residing in France) or at the French consulate (if residing abroad).
  2. The Ministry of the Interior has 12 months from filing to register or oppose the declaration.
  3. If the government does not respond within 12 months, the declaration is deemed registered.
  4. The government may oppose the declaration on grounds of indignity or lack of assimilation for up to 2 years after registration.

Important Notes:

  • Marriage alone does not confer nationality. The declaration process is required.
  • The community of life requirement is strictly enforced. Separation or divorce before the declaration is processed will result in refusal.
  • PACS (civil union) does not qualify — only marriage.
  • Same-sex marriages qualify on the same terms as opposite-sex marriages (since the 2013 law legalizing same-sex marriage).

By Naturalization (Residency)

Naturalization is a discretionary act by the French government — there is no automatic right to naturalization even if all conditions are met. It is granted by decree of the Prime Minister on the recommendation of the Ministry of the Interior.

The Assimilation Interview: The interview is conducted in French by a prefecture official. It covers:

  • Knowledge of French history (key dates, figures, events)
  • Understanding of French values (liberte, egalite, fraternite; laicite; democracy)
  • Rights and duties of French citizens (voting, taxes, jury duty, military service obligations)
  • Daily life in France and integration into French society
  • Reasons for wanting French nationality

The interview is not a formal exam with a pass/fail score. It is an assessment of overall integration and assimilation. However, poor performance will result in refusal or deferral.

Minor Children: Minor children of a naturalized person automatically acquire French nationality if they reside habitually with the newly naturalized parent (effet collectif, Article 22-1). They do not need separate applications.

Preferred Pathways

French Foreign Legion (“Francais par le sang verse”)

The French Foreign Legion has historically been one of the most distinctive pathways to French citizenship. Key details:

  • Eligibility: Any male, age 17.5-39.5, any nationality (including stateless persons)
  • Contract: Initial 5-year contract
  • Citizenship Pathway: After 3 years of service, a legionnaire may apply for naturalization with no residency requirement. In practice, the application is made through the Legion’s administrative channels and is almost always approved.
  • “French by Spilled Blood”: A legionnaire wounded in combat may immediately apply for French citizenship under an expedited procedure (“Francais par le sang verse” — French by blood spilled). There is no minimum service time for this route.
  • Identity: Foreign Legion recruits may enlist under a declared identity (nom de guerre). Upon naturalization, they may choose to retain this identity or revert to their true identity.
  • Important Notes: This pathway requires genuine military service in one of the most demanding fighting forces in the world. It is not a shortcut — it is a commitment. The Legion deploys globally and has active combat operations.

Former French Colonies / Francophone Countries

Nationals of French-speaking countries have a preferential pathway under Article 21-20:

  • The 5-year residency requirement is waived entirely for nationals of states where French is an official or customary language.
  • The applicant must still meet all other requirements (language, assimilation, good character, resources).
  • This covers a wide range of countries in Africa (Senegal, Cote d’Ivoire, Mali, Cameroon, Democratic Republic of Congo, Madagascar, etc.), as well as Haiti, Lebanon (historically), and others.

Reintegration of Former French Nationals

Persons who lost French nationality (or whose ancestors lost it) due to the independence of former French colonies may apply for reintegration:

  • Persons who had French nationality and lost it upon the independence of their country (especially in North and West Africa, Indochina, etc.) may apply for reintegration by declaration.
  • Descendants of such persons may have claims depending on the specific bilateral nationality agreements between France and the former colony.
  • The rules vary significantly by country and by the specific independence agreement (e.g., Algeria, Tunisia, Morocco, Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos, Senegal, etc.).

Special Clauses and Programs

Overseas Territories

France has several overseas departments and territories (DOMs/TOMs) with specific nationality provisions:

  • New Caledonia: Has a distinct “citizenship of New Caledonia” (citoyennete neo-caledonienne) which is separate from (and in addition to) French nationality. This local citizenship restricts voting in certain local elections and access to some local employment. French nationality law applies in full — anyone born in New Caledonia is born in France for nationality purposes.
  • Mayotte: Stricter jus soli rules since 2018 (see “By Birth” section above).
  • French Polynesia, Wallis and Futuna, Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon, Saint-Barthelemy, Saint-Martin: Standard French nationality law applies.

Effet Collectif (Collective Effect)

When a person acquires French nationality by naturalization or declaration, their minor children who reside habitually with them automatically acquire French nationality (Article 22-1). No separate application is needed. This is one of the most family-friendly provisions in European nationality law.

Declaration of French Nationality by Possession d’Etat

A person who has possessed the “status of French national” (possession d’etat de francais) for at least 10 years — meaning they have been treated as French by authorities, used French identity documents, exercised civic rights, and been considered French by those around them — may declare French nationality on the basis of this possession d’etat (Article 21-13). This is a safety net provision for persons whose nationality is unclear due to historical circumstances.

Francisation of Names

Upon acquiring French nationality, a person may request the “francisation” of their first name and/or surname — adapting it to French form (e.g., translating a first name, simplifying spelling). This is optional and is handled as part of the naturalization decree.

Investment Pathways

France does not have a formal “citizenship by investment” program. There is no direct path from financial investment to nationality. However, several residence permits facilitate an eventual path to naturalization:

Exceptional Contribution Route

Article 21-18 allows the government to reduce or waive the residency requirement for persons who have rendered “exceptional services” to France or who have exceptional talents. This is sometimes used for prominent investors, scientists, artists, or athletes. It is entirely discretionary and rare.

Business & Entrepreneurship

France has undergone a significant transformation in its startup culture over the past decade, spearheaded by government initiatives like La French Tech and the creation of Station F, the world’s largest startup campus. While bureaucracy and labor regulations remain challenges, dedicated visa programs and generous R&D tax credits make France increasingly competitive for tech entrepreneurs.

Loss of Nationality

Voluntary Renunciation

A French national may renounce French nationality by declaration if they hold another nationality (Article 23). Key rules:

  • The person must be of legal age (18+).
  • The person must hold at least one other nationality (France will not allow renunciation resulting in statelessness).
  • If the person is subject to outstanding military service obligations, renunciation may be refused.
  • Renunciation takes effect on the date the declaration is registered.
  • Government employees in certain positions (military, diplomatic, high civil service) may face restrictions.

Minors: Parents cannot renounce French nationality on behalf of their minor children. A minor may not renounce until reaching majority.

Involuntary Loss

Involuntary loss of French nationality is rare and heavily restricted:

By Government Act (Article 25): The government may strip French nationality from a person who acquired it (not from those who are French by origin) in the following cases:

  • Conviction for a crime or offense constituting an attack on the fundamental interests of the nation
  • Conviction for a crime or offense constituting an act of terrorism
  • Conviction for a crime resulting in a sentence of at least 5 years of imprisonment
  • Acting as a public servant for a foreign state and refusing to resign despite formal notice
  • Acting in a way incompatible with the status of French national and prejudicial to France’s interests

Strict Limitations:

  • Stripping of nationality cannot be applied to persons who are French from birth (nationality of origin) — only to those who acquired it.
  • It cannot render the person stateless. If the person has no other nationality, stripping is prohibited.
  • The decision must be taken within 10 years of the acquisition of nationality (extended to 15 years for terrorism-related offenses since the 2006 law).
  • The decision is made by decree after advisory opinion of the Council of State (Conseil d’Etat).

Failure to Use (Article 23-6): A person born abroad of a French parent who was also born abroad may lose French nationality if they never established any genuine connection with France (no consular registration, no French identity documents, no residence) for 50 years. This is the “desuetude” principle and is rarely applied.

Recovery / Resumption

Persons who lost French nationality may recover it through:

Declaration of Reintegration (Article 24-2):

  • Available to persons who lost French nationality by marriage to a foreign spouse (under old law), by voluntary renunciation, or by operation of law.
  • Conditions: habitual residence in France (or abroad with proven ties to France), good character, and assimilation.
  • Some categories have an automatic right to reintegration; others are subject to discretion.

Naturalization:

  • A former French national may apply for naturalization under the standard process. The residency requirement may be reduced or waived.

By Decree:

  • In exceptional cases, reintegration can be granted by decree, particularly for persons who lost nationality due to historical circumstances (colonial independence, wartime provisions).

Tips

  • Start gathering documents early. Foreign criminal records and apostilled birth certificates can take months to obtain.
  • The B1 language requirement is strictly enforced. Register for the TCF ANF or DELF B1 well in advance — exam sessions fill up quickly.
  • Prefectures vary significantly in processing speed. Paris and Ile-de-France tend to be slower. Smaller prefectures in rural departments may process faster.
  • The ANEF online portal (available at anef.immigration.gouv.fr) is increasingly the primary filing method. Check your prefecture’s specific procedures.
  • Keep every document. France’s administration values paper trails. Keep copies of all residence permits, tax notices, pay slips, and correspondence.
  • The assimilation interview is real. Study basic French history (the Revolution, the Republics, key dates), the national symbols (flag, anthem, motto), and the principles of the Republic (laicite, equality of men and women, democracy). The interview is typically 15-30 minutes.
